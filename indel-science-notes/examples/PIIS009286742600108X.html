SUMMARY Environmental DNA sequencing has revolutionized our understanding of microbial diversity and ecology. Microbiomes have now been sequenced across the entire planet—from the deep subsurface to the mountaintops—covering a myriad of hosts, biomes, and conditions. Yet, the diversity of sequencing and processing strategies hampers universal insights. MicrobeAtlas unifies more than two million microbiome samples in a single resource, harmonized to facilitate discoveries across technologies. Communities are hierarchically quantified at adjustable small subunit rRNA marker gene resolution and feature detailed metadata, including rich geographic information. Connections to the genome, phenotype, and ecological resources enable multimodal insights. Microbial lineages can be reliably tracked across environments, including a ‘‘long tail’’ of rare, uncharacterized species. Recurring community structures and geographic preferences become apparent, and global, taxonomy-specific generalism trends emerge. With MicrobeAtlas (www.microbeatlas.org), known and newly described species and communities can readily be placed into their ecological context, taking full advantage of earlier work.

INTRODUCTION

The rapidly evolving techniques of massively parallel sequencing have revolutionized microbiology. They have highlighted the enormous undescribed diversity of microbial species and provided valuable insights into microbial ecosystems.1–4 The scale and breadth of sampled environments are increasingly advancing our understanding of microbial dispersal, niches, and biogeography.5–7 However, characterizing individual roles of microbial species within their communities and across geographic boundaries remains challenging.

The gold standard for characterizing microbial species requires isolation as pure cultures, followed by metabolic and physiological characterization. This often impractical and timeconsuming approach is increasingly complemented by cultureindependent methods that assemble microbial genomes directly from environmental sequences.2,8 While these metagenomeassembled genomes (MAGs) enable first functional insights into uncultured microbes, their predictions do not always translate directly into phenotypes or ecological roles.9 Furthermore, despite recent efforts,10–13 the number of microbial species that have been cultured, phenotyped, or (meta)genomesequenced at sufficient quality is still orders of magnitude lower than the estimated total species richness.14,15 Marker gene sequencing offers an orthogonal approach to study microbes directly in their native community context. It systematically maps microbial species across environments, providing culture-independent insights into their abundance patterns, environmental conditions, and co-occurring taxa.6,7,16,17 A cornerstone of this approach is the 16S small subunit ribosomal RNA gene (16S SSU rRNA), the most universally used molecular marker for microbial lineage identification. Large reference collections now aggregate millions of these sequences,18 facilitating cross-study integration and revealing extensive microbial diversity. Despite limitations in quantifying lineage abundances,19 SSU rRNA sequencing remains a cost-effective tool for microbiome analysis.20 While phylogenetic resolution is constrained by sequence length and quality, advances in long-read technologies, such as circular-consensus and nanopore sequencing, are increasingly enabling species- and potentially even strain-level resolution.21,22 Previous marker gene studies, while restricted in scope, have proven instrumental in the characterization of the ecological preferences of microbial taxa,3,16,23 the rare biosphere,24 the ll OPEN ACCESS 2092 Cell 189, 2092–2107, April 2, 2026 © 2026 The Author(s). identification of host-associated microbial taxa characteristic of health and disease,25–28 and the identification of statistical signals indicative of biotic interactions.29–31 However, many essential phenomena, including planetary biochemical cycling, global pathogen dispersal, and climate change, operate across environmental boundaries and thus require a global perspective. Tracking microbes across multiple environments and studies remains challenging, as most existing large-scale datasets are confined to specific environments or hosts, and methods to systematically link these collections are lacking. The largest crossenvironmental study to date, the Earth Microbiome Project, analyzed over 27,000 microbial community samples,7 yet it captures only a small fraction of publicly available datasets and remains largely unintegrated with other studies. Millions of environmental and clinical microbiome samples are hosted by repositories like the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA32) and the EBI European Nucleotide Archive (ENA33), yet much of this raw data is not readily available for cross-study analyses. To help mitigate this issue, several databases and web resources have recently emerged to facilitate access to public microbiome data, typically focusing on single sequencing technologies. Some platforms analyze mainly metagenomic samples, providing high-resolution community profiles (SPIRE34,35 and Sandpiper36), sometimes combined with functional insights from MAG assembly and gene- or protein-level characterizations (SPIRE34,35 and MGnify37). A few specialize in specific environments, like the oceans (OMD38). However, due to the high cost of generating and analyzing metagenomic data, processed microbial communities remain limited to between tens of thousands and several hundred thousand per platform. Other resources (Qiita,39 MGnify, and IMNGS40) process subsets of the more abundant 16S amplicon data (up to half a million samples), but commonly applied de novo clustering or denoising techniques41–44 can lead to incompatibilities between studies analyzing different hypervariable regions of the 16S gene, hindering cross-study analyses within platforms. In addition, most resources focus on named taxa or fixed operational taxonomic units (OTUs), limiting the exploration of undescribed diversity at different taxonomic scales.

Here, we present MicrobeAtlas, an integrated resource of 2,390,937 analyzed microbial communities from all around the globe. We mapped these microbiome samples against a reference database with over 1.5 million full-length 16S and 18S SSU rRNA sequences, pre-clustered hierarchically into hundreds of thousands of OTUs at selectable granularity. Mapping to a common, full-length reference enables OTUs and communities to be tracked across sequencing strategies (metagenomic and amplicon) and protocols (e.g., amplified 16S region) throughout the entire resource. Extracted metadata keywords, informative environmental variables (e.g., pH and temperature), and two-level environmental ontology assignments are available, complemented with carefully extracted geographic coordinates and spatial connections to terrestrial ecosystem definitions by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN45). Communities are hierarchically grouped into community clusters, providing a bird’s-eye view on global community patterns and potential niche distributions. While the focus of MicrobeAtlas lies on prokaryotic analyses, it also tracks eukaryotic OTUs across hundreds of thousands of communities, providing a base for integrating microbial eukaryotes into ecosystem studies. Analyzing a robust subset of more than one million MicrobeAtlas samples reveals intriguing patterns: communities are broadly structured by environment but also according to diverse, informative keywords related to specific conditions and hosts. Soil and aquatic microbiomes emerge as global richness hotspots with many poorly characterized members, contrasting most animal microbiomes. Individual OTUs can be reliably traced across the globe, revealing detailed habitat generalism and specialism trends, as well as geographic preferences. This includes the uncharted majority of OTUs without cultured representatives, sequenced genomes, or robust taxonomic assignments, revealing first glimpses of their ecology. Finally, we identified blind spots in current sampling efforts that point to geographic regions, ecological lifestyles, and specialized biomes that require more concerted attention by the community. In summary, MicrobeAtlas enables globe-spanning microbiomics at an unprecedented scale, catalyzing new insights into Earth’s microbial ecosystems.

RESULTS

The MicrobeAtlas dataset We comprehensively screened the NCBI SRA database for microbial community sequencing samples and downloaded raw reads and metadata for 2,390,937 samples (52,950 studies).

This screen was purposefully inclusive, selecting any sequencing projects with metadata keywords matching ‘‘metagen*,’’ ‘‘microbi*,’’ ‘‘bacteria,’’ or ‘‘archaea,’’ and thus covering microbial data from 16S, 18S, and metagenomic experiments across diverse technologies (e.g., amplicon and whole-genome shotgun [WGS]). Utilizing a sequencing strategy-agnostic workflow, we quality-checked and mapped 6.87 trillion raw reads against a custom database of 1,536,850 full-length 16S/18S marker gene sequences. This resulted in the identification of 214 billion SSU rRNA sequences across all samples (discarding non-SSU reads, e.g., from WGS experiments), which subsequently underwent OTU assignment. After removing samples with low community complexity (<20 OTUs), low sequencing depth (<1,000 SSU reads), or low estimated coverage (90%; see STAR Methods), we obtained a dataset of 1,153,349 consistent, comparable community abundance profiles (24,349 studies) used for analyses in this study (Figure 1).

How different community members are defined and distinguished can profoundly impact ecological analyses. To enable flexible definitions, we therefore clustered our reference sequences into hierarchical OTUs with 90% to 99% sequence identity (33,361 to 241,196 OTUs, 651,275 OTUs total, default: 97%-OTUs) and assigned community profiles for each OTU level to all samples. While the current focus is on bacteria, our reference includes 6,884 to 38,321 18S-based and 6,015 to 24,559 16S-based eukaryotic OTUs (per OTU level; 16S-based: mainly mitochondria and chloroplasts), as well as 1,179 to 9,439 archaeal OTUs, all of which are tracked across the resource. A key component of MicrobeAtlas is its rich metadata. For this purpose, we extracted information on environments, geo-locations, and informative keywords from raw metadata to provide detailed biological and technological context for each community. We categorized samples into a simple, two-tiered environmental ontology (Table S1) and, where possible, additionally linked terrestrial samples geographically to the IUCN Global Ecosystem Typology, providing more precise ecosystem definitions based on macro-ecological characteristics (Table S2). The resulting dataset spans four main environments (animal, aquatic, soil, and plant) and 65 sub-environments, covering a plethora of habitats, hosts, and conditions from all continents. In addition, we linked MicrobeAtlas OTUs to culture collections (BacDive46) and genome clusters (proGenomes347), connecting ecological distributions with phenotypic and genetic information. To our knowledge, MicrobeAtlas houses the largest and most diverse collection of cross-study comparable microbial community profiles available to date. To enable intuitive exploration of this dataset, we created a feature-rich website (www.microbeatlas.org) that allows the tracking, comparison, and download of studies, samples, and individual taxa from around the globe (Figure 1). OTUs can be searched based on taxonomic or metadata matches and can be visualized on a global map, together with their abundance distributions across environments. Users can map SSU sequences directly on the website to explore their own microbes of interest in a global context. Similarly, samples and projects can be identified using rich metadata search and explored in terms of community composition, keywords, and technical information across flexible OTU definitions.

Among larger projection clusters, we observed detailed patterns, where neighboring yet distinct sub-clusters differ in both microbial composition and metadata annotation in biologically interpretable ways. Using keyword analysis, we characterized a freshwater projection cluster in more detail and distinguished it from two marine clusters, one related to benthic and sediment communities and the other dominated by planktonic and hostassociated communities (Figure 2A, bottom). These clusters also differ in community composition at the class level, with marine clusters being more similar to each other than to the freshwater cluster (class-level Bray-Curtis: 0.25 vs. 0.42). In addition, we found examples of highly localized keywords (Figure 2C) distinguishing projection regions enriched for certain soil types (tundra and farm soil), hosts (horse and bird), or conditions (high-fat diet). We also identified environmental gradients in a subset of clusters: peatland/forest and field samples clearly separated by pH (Pearson’s ρ = 0.43), while a major marine cluster was stratified by temperature (ρ = 0.30) and physical sampling depth (ρ = 0.34) (p = 0.001 [all tests], Mantel test [Bray-Curtis]) (Figures S1K–S1O). A more targeted extraction of host information via host-specific fields and taxonomic IDs recovered 2,682 host species, the most frequent ones often localized in specific regions of the projection (Figures S1P and S1S). Mouse was the second most frequent host (after human) (Figure S1R), and notably, mouse gut communities formed a separate cluster in our projection (Figures 2A and S1Q). Closer examination of semantic keyword groups (STAR Methods) revealed enrichment of categories related to (experimental) perturbation and laboratory conditions in this cluster, including terms such as ‘‘antibiotics,’’ ‘‘disruption,’’ ‘‘heat-killed,’’ ‘‘dose-dependent,’’ and ‘‘stress’’ (Table S3). By contrast, the core human gut cluster was closer to non-mouse hosts in the projection and featured more observational keyword groups related to subject traits or diseases, such as ‘‘female,’’ ‘‘twin,’’ ‘‘infant,’’ ‘‘malnutrition,’’ and ‘‘ulcer’’ (Table S3; Figure S1Q).

In line with previous studies, we found that environments differ strongly in diversity. Estimated mean OTU richness per community cluster (Figure 2E) was particularly high in environmental (soil, freshwater, and marine; mean 715–1,257 OTUs), wastewater (1,142 OTUs), and ruminant animal samples (988 OTUs), independent of sequencing technology (Figure S2A).

By contrast, we found lower richness in other animals (mean 490 OTUs; in particular, insects with 281 OTUs) and surface plant organs (539 OTUs). Counting observed OTUs after rarefaction or using a stricter sequencing depth cutoff (>=5,000 SSU reads) led to the same qualitative conclusions (Figures S2B and S2C). In terms of taxonomic coverage, human samples had high fractions of reads classified at least to the genus level (mean 78.4%) (Figure 2F), while environmental communities had strongly reduced fractions (down to mean 35%), indicating notable under-characterization of these high-richness habitats (possibly due to underrepresentation in sampling and reference collection efforts50,51). This was particularly apparent for soils, sediments, and marine samples (37.2% and 35.9%), but also freshwater (45.7%), and, intriguingly, mouse communities (42.7%) (Figure S2D). In general, classification fraction and richness were negatively correlated across community clusters (Pearson’s ρ = −0.36, p < 10−100) (Figure S2E). Community clustering can provide an unsupervised way to detect distinct community types that reoccur across different hosts and conditions. To this end, we identified examples of community clusters with possible ecological interpretations. For instance, cluster SGRP1254 (1,529 samples, 32 projects) is dominated by five different Bifidobacterium OTUs (level: 97%) and is annotated with keywords related to infants and birth (Figure S2F), in line with established colonization patterns in breast-fed children.52 Intriguingly, this cluster also includes samples from monkeys, partially annotated with breastfeeding- and probiotic-related terms (Figure S2H).

Similarly, cluster SGRP3859 (833 samples, 42 projects) predominantly features typical bee-gut-related genera (e.g., Giliamella, Snodgrassella, and Frischella53) and is annotated with bee- or bumblebeerelated terms. Notably, this cluster also includes plant leaf samples (potentially contaminated with bee feces) and fecal communities from swallows (known predators of bees) (Figure S2G), again highlighting how community types can be traced across diverse hosts and conditions.

Finally, we also identified technology-associated effects. A minor region of the projection, comprising community clusters from diverse environments, was strongly enriched for eukaryotes (mean eukaryotic fraction: 88.5%) (Figure S2I), particularly fungi (29.3%) and metazoa (17.5%). The majority of samples (97.2%) in these localized clusters were generated using eukaryote-specific 18S primers, while the remainder used WGS or 16S amplification (primarily 16S from mitochondria and chloroplasts), indicating technological effects driving this clustering. Globally, we identified 64,130 samples with eukaryotic fractions >90% (and 176,564 samples with fractions >10%), covering diverse environments and technologies and providing a broad foundation for potential eukaryote-targeted analyses.

Beyond eukaryote-specific patterns, we also observed signs of localized clustering among metagenomic samples (i.e., generated using WGS protocols) (Figure S2J) compared with amplicon samples (Figure S2K). However, these effects were small compared with the main environment signals described above (PERMANOVA [unifrac-w]: R2 = 0.6%, p = 0.001).

By contrast, the vast majority of OTUs in MicrobeAtlas (n = 99,241, 88.7%) lacked cultured isolates, genomes, and detailed taxonomy (Figures 3A–3C), making them potential representatives of ‘‘microbial dark matter’’54 (i.e., uncharted taxonomic branches with potentially—but not necessarily—unique functions and ecology). As expected, OTUs without any culture strain or genome information (ncg-OTUs) tended to be less taxonomically classified: only 21.6% had confident consensus assignments at the genus level (class: 83.0%), whereas OTUs with culture strain or genome annotations (cgOTUs) reached 65.4% (class: 93.0%) (Figure 3B). Notably, 14,985 ncg-OTUs were classified only at the phylum level or higher, outnumbering all cg-OTUs combined (12,543). Among them, 4,988 ncg-OTUs (5%) lacked any consensus phylum annotation and may potentially represent uncharacterized phyla. In addition, the vast majority of described (candidate) phyla were dominated by ncg-OTUs, while only around half of the described genera showed this trend (Figure 3C).

MicrobeAtlas allowed us to globally track both taxonomically characterized and uncharacterized OTUs to gain insights into their ecology. We observed a notable number of ncg-OTUs among the most abundant microbes across environments (17 out of the top 150), despite cg-OTUs being on average 16 times more abundant than ncg-OTUs (Figure 3D). Top ncg-OTUs included many Bacteroidales OTUs (e.g., Muribaculaceae and Bacteroides) (Table S4), but also an archaeal OTU classified as Candidatus Nitrosocosmicus, detected in 82,660 samples (4,232 studies) across all continents (Figure S3A) and most prevalent in soil (56%). Notably, several OTUs without phylum annotations also reached high abundances (up to the 97th percentile) (Figure 3E), for instance, OTU 97_17665 with 16,222 samples (2,736 studies), most abundant in drinking-water-associated communities and biofilms (Figure S3B).

Out of 99 bacterial and archaeal phyla, 43 featured exclusively phylum-level OTUs, and only a minority of phyla were on average classified at the family or genus level (26 and 2 phyla, respectively) (Figure S3C). Each OTU in MicrobeAtlas is part of a nested hierarchy generated by agglomerative clustering of 16S sequences (Figure 1). This structure allows exploration of microbial lineages at varying sequence identity thresholds (e.g., 97%–99%) and enables coarse-grained eco-evolutionary analyses of largely uncharacterized OTUs (Figure 3G). For instance, we found an example of notable environmental separation within a 97%-OTU classified only as Candidatus Bathyarchaeota (Figure 3G). This OTU and most of its children were predominantly present in soil-like environments, with minor occurrences in aquatic samples. However, one child OTU at the 99% level (99_191556, found in 314 samples from 171 studies) specialized toward predominantly aquatic environments, while one of its sister OTUs (99_194944, 474 samples, 165 studies) was encountered almost exclusively in soils. Comparative keyword analysis indicated 99_191556 to be more abundant in limnic and wetland environments, while 99_194944 seems to thrive in peaty soils (Figure 3G). We also detected an intriguing case of geographic separation: the uncharacterized OTU 90_1226 (Candidatus Marinimicrobia, OTU level: 90%) is widespread in oceans around the globe, but several of its 99%-level children show notable latitudinal specificity. For instance, 99_13913 (2,437 samples, 250 projects) is enriched in northern oceans (mainly subarctic), while 99_36550 (5,303 samples, 228 projects) is specific to the equator, and 99_135986 (1,021 samples, 107 projects) is mainly found in southern subantarctic oceans (Figure S3F).

Nonetheless, we found that MicrobeAtlas provides highly improved geographic coverage (61% of global 9◦× 9◦latitude-longitude bins covered) compared with the Earth Microbiome Project (15% coverage) (Figures S3K and S3L), with a substantially broader coverage of continents and world oceans (Figures S3M and S3N).

The integration of IUCN hierarchical ecosystem definitions enabled us to comprehensively compare microbiomes between distinct macro-habitats across scales, defined in terms of function, vegetation type, or anthropogenic pressures (Figure 4B). MicrobeAtlas features diverse microbiomes from all seven terrestrial IUCN biomes, as well as 34 ecosystem functional groups (EFGs), ranging from tropical forests and grasslands to deserts and cryogenic zones. We found that the majority of the 65,909 IUCN-mappable terrestrial samples originate from heavily anthropogenic habitats (‘‘intensive land-use’’ biome [75.7%], in particular the EFG ‘‘urban and industrial ecosystems’’ [17.6%]), followed by the biomes ‘‘savannas and grasslands’’ (9.6%) and ‘‘temperate-boreal forests and woodlands’’ (7.7%) (Figure S3I; Tables S2 and S3). By contrast, the ten leastsampled EFGs (less than 10 samples each) are spread across multiple biomes and include ‘‘ice sheets, glaciers, and perpetual snowfields’’ and multiple EFGs related to heathlands and tropical or alpine environments (Figure S3J). Notably, six of these had no mapped samples (e.g., ‘‘cool temperate heathlands’’ and ‘‘hummock savannas’’). A less stringent keyword-based search recovered more samples from underrepresented environments (e.g., 5,638 samples for ‘‘glacial’’ and 20 samples for ‘‘hummock’’); however, these lacked robust geographic links to IUCN-defined ecoregions.

To further highlight the geographic richness of MicrobeAtlas, we performed a more detailed analysis of soil microbiomes from global savannas (EFGs: ‘‘T4.1 trophic savannas’’ and ‘‘T4.2 pyric tussock savannas’’), covering all continents containing savannas. To further increase ecosystem resolution, we mapped these samples to 22 distinct biogeographic ecoregions (e.g., ‘‘Drakensberg grasslands’’ and ‘‘Edwards Plateau savanna’’; as defined in Dinerstein et al.55). We found that microbial communities were strongly structured by ecoregion (PERMANOVA [Bray-Curtis]: R2 = 39.9%, p = 10−4), with ecoregions often, but not always, clustering geographically, indicating a mix of regional and environmental factors driving community composition (Figures S4A and S4B). To further explore this, we investigated five environmental variables (vegetation cover, total organic carbon, soil water index [SWI], burned area [BA], and clay content [CC]—as inferred by remote sensing and extrapolation resources56–59) and found that all of these were significantly associated with microbiome structure (Mantel test’s, ρ = 0.09– 0.37, q < 0.05 for all tests) (Figures S4C and S4D), with SWI showing the strongest and BA the overall weakest effect. We also identified local patterns: burned area showed a particularly strong localized signal for Arnhem Land and Cape York Peninsula tropical savannas (both in Australia) (Figures S4B and S4E), while environmental factors in Edwards Plateau samples (North America) showed a unique, mosaic contrast with neighboring clusters (e.g., SWI similar to the Southeast US ecoregion, but CC closer to Hawai’i tropical savannas) (Figures S4B, S4F, and S4G). In addition, environmental variables helped explain sub-structure within ecoregions: for instance, ‘‘Drakensberg grasslands’’ (Africa) microbiomes were split across three distinct clusters—all in geographic vicinity—that coincided with gradients in vegetation cover, total organic carbon, and clay content (Figures S4B and S4G–S4I).

When investigating biogeographic trends at the OTU level, we found striking examples of global niche separation. For instance, two OTUs classified as Candidatus Pelagibacter and Candidatus Fonsibacter, respectively (both Pelagibacterales), are globally prevalent in aquatic environments and occasionally geographically co-localized (Figure 4C). Yet, they show a clear environmental distinction where Pelagibacter is widely distributed across global oceans, while Fonsibacter is almost exclusively found in freshwater environments, in particular lakes (Figure 4D). In terms of broader biogeographic trends, we identified 37,393 97% OTUs with significant latitudinal preferences in at least one environment: 93.3% of these were more abundant around the equator (e.g., unclassified Rhodospirillales and Candidatus Marinimicrobia OTUs) (Figure 4E), and the remainder were more abundant in polar regions (e.g., Polaromonas and Ilumatobacter OTUs) (Figure 4F). We observed most latitudinal trends in aquatic samples (54% of significant correlations), followed by animal (31%) and soil (14%), and very little in plants (2%, possibly due to undersampling). Notably, soil samples harbored both equatorial- and polar-associated microbes (63% equatorial), in contrast to all other environments (86%–99% equatorial correlations). These results were robust to conservative latitudinal downsampling (STAR Methods). In line with high equatorial associations in aquatic OTUs (97%), marine OTU richness also peaked near the equator (max. richness bin: 0◦N to 20◦N) (Figure 4G), consistent with latitudinal species diversity gradients observed for many multicellular organisms60 and matching previous reports on marine microbes.3 Interestingly, we observed comparable diversity spikes in sufficiently sampled terrestrial biomes as well (max. richness bin: 20◦N to 40◦N, biomes: "temperate-boreal forests and woodlands," "deserts and semi-deserts," and "intensive land-use") (Figures 4B, S4L, and S4M), despite soil OTUs showing fewer equatorial associations individually than marine OTUs. Counting observed OTUs after rarefaction led to the same qualitative conclusions for both marine and terrestrial habitats (Figures S4J and S4N), as did conservative latitudinal downsampling (Figures S4K and S4O) and removal of suspected artificial microcosms (marine only) (Figure S4W). Binned absolute latitudes were correlated with temperature, as expected, but also with physical sampling depth, both in marine and terrestrial datasets (Pearson’s ρ between −0.8 and −0.99, p < 0.05 (all tests)). Marine models identified temperature as the main explanatory variable for marine diversity (R2 = 82.4%), highly collinear with latitude (ΔR2 [beyond latitude] = 8.6%), while physical sampling depth explained moderate variation alone (R2 = 38.3%) and little beyond latitude (ΔR2 = 1.6%). At this level of environmental granularity, we found that OTUs show a clear tendency toward habitat specialism (39.6% of OTUs have scores in the bottom quarter of the range, but only 10.3% lie in the top quarter) (Figure 5B, top histogram), mostly thriving in aquatic and, to a lesser extent, animal habitats (Figure 5B, bottom stacked bar charts). Intriguingly, we found oxygen tolerance (inferred for 3,483 OTUs through BacDive) (STAR Methods) to be a major potential driver of specialism and generalism (Figure S5D): anaerobes showed higher fractions of specialists—almost exclusively in aquatic and animal communities (Figure S5E)—while aerobes tended to thrive across diverse environments (Figure S5F).

On average, top generalists inhabit significantly more community clusters than top specialists (1,239.5 vs. 263.9, p < 10−99, Mann-Whitney U test) (Figure 5G), indicating wider dispersal overall. However, within community clusters (proxies for occupied niches), highly generalist and specialist OTUs (top and bottom 10%) both had similar mean abundances (4.8 × 10−5 vs. 9.2 × 10−5) and prevalences (0.546 vs. 0.549) (Figures 5D and 5E), suggesting comparable colonization success within these realized niches. Notably, specialist OTUs exhibited greater variation in both metrics: some specialists dominated their niches more than comparable generalists, while other specialists showed the opposite pattern (Figures 5D and 5E). In addition, we found clear signals of culturing biases: generalists were significantly more likely to have cultured representatives than specialists (odds ratio = 6.85, p < 10−99, chi-squared test) (Figures 5D and 5E), and in addition, more abundant and prevalent species were also significantly more likely to have been cultured (AUC = 0.72 and 0.66, p < 10−99 for both, MannWhitney U test). In terms of taxonomy, we observed notable family-level shifts across the generalism gradient (Figure 5F). Specialist OTUs cover known host-associated families, most notably Lachnospiraceae and Prevotellaceae, but also environmental (in particular aquatic) groups such as Desulfobacteraceae. By contrast, high-scoring generalists include families known for their adaptability and dispersal potential, most notably Pseudomonadaceae and Bacillaceae. Despite these trends, each investigated family harbored OTUs from both sides of the generalism spectrum, and some families showed a remarkably balanced distribution of generalists and specialists (e.g., Oscillospiraceae and Flavobacteriaceae). These patterns remained after equalized downsampling per environment (Figure S5G).

Generalists may differ from specialists in terms of genomic characteristics (e.g., genome size and number of genes), potentially providing increased flexibility in the face of varying environmental pressures. Indeed, when we investigated the average number of genes per genome within each OTU (available for 7,427 97% OTUs, based on our 16S-based mapping to proGenomes3), we found that gene counts increased consistently with higher generalism scores. Counts peaked for OTUs with generalism scores between 0.6 and 0.7, averaging 3,935.6 genes (mean), a significant 36.4% increase compared with OTUs with generalism scores between 0.0 and 0.1 (p = 10−46, MannWhitney U test). In line with earlier observations (Figures 5B and 5C), plant- and soil-associated OTUs in general tended to have larger genomes than animal and aquatic OTUs (Figure S5H). To account for this, we sub-sampled each generalism bin for equal environmental representation, resulting in a similar gene count increase that, however, plateaued earlier and reached lower statistical significance (32.7% increase, p = 10⁻⁴) (Figure S5I).

DISCUSSION

Here, we present an initial analysis of MicrobeAtlas, featuring 2,390,937 processed samples—the largest collection of crossstudy compatible microbial community profiles to date. The vast majority of community-level diversity in MicrobeAtlas comes from smaller studies, underscoring the value of integrating data beyond major consortium efforts. We complemented the dataset with systematically extracted and processed metadata information, combined with links to genome and strain collections. The entire database, including community profiles, OTU-level data, and metadata, is available as a comprehensive package, downloadable and interactively explorable on our feature-rich website at www.microbeatlas.org.

In our global analysis, we found both expected and new ecological patterns of interest. The notable fine structure we observed among major environmental microbiome clusters indicates rich, partially undiscovered sources of microbial variation. While we identified potential contributors—such as detailed sub-environmental patterns, environmental gradients, and diverse hostrelated structures—these examples merely scratch the surface. Nonetheless, they indicate the pervasiveness of niche partitioning and species sorting across geographic and environmental scales (consistent with metacommunity theory61–63) and illustrate the depth and explorative potential of MicrobeAtlas. Importantly, these patterns were partially identified through keyword frequency analysis and semantic clustering, which we see as powerful explorative tools to detect potential community drivers within non-standardized metadata. As one example, a distinct mouse gut cluster showed low taxonomic resolution, consistent with prior reports of under-characterized microbiota in both lab and wild mice.64 Frequent experiment-related terms and semantic clusters suggest lab conditions may contribute to this separation. These microbiomes also differ markedly from human gut communities, ll OPEN ACCESS Cell 189, 2092–2107, April 2, 2026 2101 Resource

highlighting ongoing concerns about the relevance of mouse models.65,66 Biogeography is a major focus of MicrobeAtlas, supported by automatically extracted, cleaned coordinates, environmental variables, and IUCN ecosystem annotations. The widespread latitudinal trends we detected across taxa include evidence for (sub-)tropical diversity hotspots in marine environments, expanding on earlier reports3,6 and contributing to long-standing discussions of latitudinal diversity gradients in macro-ecology.60,67 Our analysis of intercontinental savannas points toward a complex mix of globally consistent environmental and region-specific factors shaping grass- and woodland microbiomes, potentially reflecting regional context dependence63,68 or, more speculatively, historical contingencies and species priority effects.69,70 In addition, indications of sharp niche separation between two geographically co-localized species,71 along with clear latitudinal divides within a single Candidatus Marinimicrobia OTU, further illustrate the breadth of avenues for geographic analysis and ecotype discovery.72,73 Our quantification of high-level habitat generalism covers hundreds of thousands of OTUs across more than one million communities, providing a useful resource for the research community. The strong generalism-specialism segregation we observed among taxonomic families coincides with established host associations (e.g., Lachnospiraceae74) and resilient, broadly adaptable groups (e.g., Pseudomonadaceae75 and Bacillaceae76) at opposite sides of the spectrum. Notably, Enterobacteriaceae—harboring notorious pathogens such as Enterobacter, Salmonella, and Klebsiella—exhibit concerningly high generalism scores, suggestive of broad environmental reservoirs.77,78 The enrichment of generalists in soils and plants/ rhizospheres is in line with these exceptionally structured and fluctuating environments,79,80 benefitting highly adaptable species,81,82 while more homeostatic (animal gut) or homogeneous (aquatic) habitats appear to favor specialism. The tendency of generalists toward larger genomes suggests more flexible metabolic strategies81 and expanded utility functions (e.g., siderophores and antimicrobials), in line with previous studies83,84 and further supporting enhanced environmental robustness. In addition, increased oxygen tolerance in these taxa could indicate wider dispersal potential.81,85 Our investigation of global microbiomes revealed notable dark spots in global study efforts. The vast majority of MicrobeAtlas OTUs remain largely uncharacterized, lacking cultures, genomes, and detailed taxonomy. Among them, we identified numerous highly abundant yet understudied OTUs thriving in environmental samples, emphasizing the need for further microbial characterization in global soils, lakes, rivers, and oceans. We found culturing biases to particularly favor habitat generalists and abundant OTUs, stressing the importance of highthroughput culturing efforts targeting a broader microbial spectrum86—critical, for instance, in bioactive compound discovery.38 With keyword-based metadata, aided by web-based exploration and easy data extraction for co-occurrence and other analyses, MicrobeAtlas offers data-driven ways to prioritize these enigmatic yet likely impactful taxa. Major gaps also persist at the environmental and geographic levels. Animal microbiomes are notably over-represented, making up over 50% of all samples, whereas marine samples, for instance, account for less than 8%, despite oceans covering the majority of Earth. While animal studies are of clinical importance, understanding global environment-critical phenomena such as biogeochemical cycles and climate change requires similar characterization efforts for environmental communities.87 We also found sampling biases across (bio)geographic axes: while sampling epicenters in North America, Europe, and East Asia become apparent, other regions are notably under-represented, including most climate zones (in particular tropical and polar zones), ecosystems (e.g., alpine regions and savannas), and the Southern Hemisphere in general. Exploring understudied environments, regions, and traditional cultural practices continues to reveal new biological insights,54,88 underscoring the importance of targeted sampling efforts in such blind spots. This is further illustrated by our cross-continental analysis of savanna types, often un- or undersampled in large surveys (e.g., the EMP), which adds rich, global context to otherwise localized observations.

In the future, MicrobeAtlas will continue to receive regular updates, with the upcoming release scheduled to comprise 6.3 million samples, currently in the final stages of processing. Looking forward, the resource will benefit from the extension of SSU rRNA reference collections, aided by long-read sequencing technologies.22,89 Emerging machine learning tools that extract information from noisy metadata90,91 should further improve sample categorization, complementing increasingly consistent metadata standards.92 Cross-linking MicrobeAtlas to Earth observation data would provide an intriguing interdisciplinary avenue to increase data richness and extend global prediction models with microbial information.93 We are only beginning to unravel the intricate network of global microbial ecosystems, but data-driven resources such as MicrobeAtlas will be key to advancing that goal. Limitations of the study Our integrative reference-based approach quantifies only inreference community members, which can hamper the detection of undersampled microbial groups. While our reference shows broad coverage of genome collections and is therefore a good representation of the current state of knowledge, future reference updates will likely increase community resolution, in particular of more exotic environments. Similarly, if reference-independent approaches (e.g., based on Amplicon Sequence Variants [ASVs]94) can be extended to integrate data across different amplified regions, they may allow insights into the unreferenced microsphere in the future. Taxonomic classification of microbial eukaryotes is still lagging behind prokaryotes, and integration of genomic data (beyond 18S) and extended annotation efforts should facilitate eukaryote-focused and cross-domain analyses moving forward. Furthermore, our generalism scores are based on coarse-grained habitat classifications. While bias-free categorization is challenging, existing ontologies could be customized in the future to integrate more nuanced environmental gradients (e.g., aquatic: marine vs. freshwater) into generalism quantification. Finally, metadata is notoriously heterogeneous and often incomplete, restricting analyses to data subsets with confident annotations. Sample metadata extraction and processing The NCBI SRA provides biological metadata for samples, but quality and detail of annotations vary across studies since they depend on data submitters. To standardize this metadata, keywords were extracted from multiple annotation fields (isolation source, disease status, host information, body habitat/site, environmental package, and tissue annotations) and processed through string normalization and filtering steps, including lowercasing, removal of special characters, and tokenization. In addition, custom rules for term disambiguation, plural forms and synonyms were implemented and applied. The resulting controlled vocabulary was used to classify samples into four main environments: animal, plant, aquatic, and soil. Samples were assigned to main environments by matching their standardized keywords against environment-specific term sets (e.g., ‘‘leaf, banana, tree, crop’’ matches only ‘‘plant’’; automatic assignment: ‘‘plant’’) (Table S5). When samples had annotations matching multiple environments, they were labelled as "unknown" (e.g., ‘‘leaf, banana, tree, insect’’ matches both ‘‘animal’’ and ‘‘plant’’; automatic assignment: ‘‘unknown’’). To extract and harmonize environmental metadata (pH, temperature and depth), field labels that matched corresponding search patterns were extracted, sorted by frequency of occurrence, and then manually reviewed (initial search patterns were: ph, temp*, dept*; all case-insensitive). Field labels that matched initially but are not desirable (such as ‘temporal’ instead of ‘temperature’) were blocked. Subsequently, numerical values and units of measurements were parsed from the corresponding field values and converted as necessary. In the case of temperature, for example, all three possible units (Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin) were encountered, and at least 15 distinct special characters or abbreviations that can express ‘degrees’. Ambiguous annotations assigning more than one value to a given sample were excluded.

Host species were initially assigned by matching relevant keywords and, for more quantitative analyses, through explicit parsing of dedicated metadata fields containing host names and taxonomic IDs. To this end, relevant metadata fields were detected using regular expressions matching variations of ‘‘host’’, ‘‘name’’ and ‘‘taxid’’. Identified fields were manually checked and added to a white list, accounting for misspelling, word separation and capitalization. Entries from white-listed fields for all samples were then parsed and homogenized by translating identified names to NCBI taxonomic IDs via the ete3 toolkit110 v3.1. Samples with multiple conflicting host assignments were treated as unassigned.

OTU clustering and taxonomic classification The full-length SSU rRNA reference database MAPref 2.2b, delivered with MAPseq97 v1.2.6, was used to define and taxonomically classify OTUs. Briefly, the database contained 1,536,850 quality-filtered full-length 16S and 18S sequences, extracted from the NCBI RefSeq and Genbank databases. Initial quality filtering was performed by removing sequences smaller than 1000bp, larger than 3500bp, with more than 2% unknown nucleotides (N) or two such nucleotides in a row (NN). Sequences were then aligned using INFERNAL114 v1.1.2 with three rRNA models for Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. The alignment of every sequence against all three models was compared and each sequence was classified as Bacteria, Archaea or Eukaryota based on its best alignment score against each of the models. Only nucleotides aligned to the models were considered (insertions were removed), and sequences were trimmed between two well-conserved alignment columns found to cover a large fraction of the aligned sequences for each class. Sequences not covering the full length to within 10bp between the trimmed positions were filtered out, as were sequences with more than 30% gaps in the whole aligned region. Chimeric sequences were removed by running UCHIME115 against a reference set of sequences confirmed by at least three independent studies and falling within the same OTUs (defined at 98%). Based on this alignment, sequences were then hierarchically clustered using HPC-CLUST98 v1.2 at different sequence identity thresholds (90%, 96%, 97%, 98%, and 99%), resulting in hierarchical OTU definitions (parents and children). Taxonomies for each OTU were assigned based on a 90% consensus annotation across all of its sequences, obtained either directly via NCBI RefSeq and the All-Species Living Tree Project taxonomic annotations (‘‘gold set’’) or indirectly by mapping un-annotated sequences to the gold set (‘‘silver set’’). For details, see 97.

In total, MAPref 2.2b features 1,425,265 16S - including 31,950 from archaea and 16,493 from eukaryotic organelles - and 111,585 18S sequences. These were mapped to 90 (15238) bacterial, 10 (657) archaeal, and 66 (2126) eukaryotic phyla (species). MAPref 2.2b covers 97.3% out of 754k genomes that contain at least one assembled 16S in proGenomes3, suggesting broad coverage of both major and minor taxonomic groups. In addition, earlier versions were benchmarked favorably on mock communities97 (MAPref 2.0) and diverse synthetic data sets117 (MAPref 2.2).

Raw read quality filtering and OTU assignment Fastq sequencing data was extracted from downloaded SRA sequencing runs using the fastq-dump provided by NCBI. A custom C++ program was used to filter and trim the reads using the quality information provided, based on the following criteria: base calls were defined as low quality when they had a quality score below 10, reads were trimmed when two consecutive low quality base calls were identified, and reads shorter than 75bp or with a fraction of low quality base calls higher than 5% were discarded entirely. To compute OTU and taxonomic profiles for millions of sequencing samples, MAPseq97 was used: a high-performance read mapper previously developed by the authors. In brief, when mapping reads to a reference, MAPseq first performs a fast kmer pre-filtering approach on non-redundant, pre-clustered reference sequences to heuristically detect promising OTU clusters. In a second step, it then expands the sequences from the identified clusters and efficiently computes full pairwise local alignments. Finally, it ranks hits by alignment score and computes confidences for all OTU and taxon labels, taking into consideration sequence identity and score similarities between the best and second best hits for each label. Using MAPseq, all quality-filtered reads were assigned to the NCBI taxonomy and to OTU labels at different identity cutoffs (90%, 96%, 97%, 98%, and 99%). A confidence cutoff of 0.5 was used to obtain confident read assignments; reads with alignment scores lower than 30 were discarded.

For the present study, samples with less than 20 OTUs (OTU level: 96%), with less than 1000 SSU reads, or not annotated as ‘‘source=METAGENOMIC’’ were discarded to reduce the fraction of lower-quality or non-microbiome samples, resulting in 1,153,349 remaining samples. The 20 OTUs threshold was informed by an earlier analysis118 of the authors which found that samples below this threshold showed noisy environmental annotations in a global dataset of human body sites (>15,000 samples, >50 studies). The 1000 SSU filter aims to remove ill-behaved samples (e.g., failed experimental protocols, misannotated non-metagenomic samples). This dataset served as the base for community clustering (see below). For per-sample downstream analyses, samples with less than 90% estimated community coverage119 (based on 97% OTUs) were additionally removed, yielding a dataset of 1,039,362 samples. Targeted contaminant removal based on established lists120 was considered, but finally decided against because distinguishing genuine microbial diversity from lab contamination is not always consistent and reliable in a dataset as heterogeneous as MicrobeAtlas. For several sanity checks, we conservatively removed suspected artificial and anthropogenic microcosms by dropping samples annotated with any of the following keywords: antibiotic, perturbed, gnotobiotic, heat-killed, disturbance, exposure, treatment, subject, probiotic, cultivation, transplantation, waste water, sludge, anthropogenic, diabetes mellitus, inflammation, storage, contaminated, personalized, inoculation, bioreactor, consortium, antimicrobial, dosage, vaccination, sewage, biogas, vancomycin, industry, pesticide, polluted, germfree, drug, contaminated soil, irradiation, dose-dependent, plastic, antibiotic-perturbed, engraftment, antibiotic-treated, antibiotic-mediated, petroleum, biofuel, dose-response, radioactivity, agrochemical, clinical (manually curated from the top 1000 most frequent keywords). Community clustering For all samples with at least 20 OTUs and 1000 SSU reads, pairwise microbial community distances were calculated (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity on log-transformed relative abundances), followed by average linkage hierarchical clustering (dissimilarity threshold: 0.5). To handle the computational demand of >1 million samples, a modified version of HPC-CLUST98 v1.2 was used. While different research questions may require different clustering cutoffs, sensitivity analyses showed that the Bray-Curtis threshold of 0.5 globally provided a good trade-off between cluster granularity (higher number of clusters, suggestive of increased fine-structure) and robustness (samples per cluster, improving per-cluster estimates and annotations; see below) (Figure S5J). For the chosen threshold, we obtained 356,182 community clusters, which were further reduced to a more robust and computationally tractable subset of 36,358 clusters encompassing at least five distinct samples each. More specifically, this filtering was performed to reduce variation and noise of OTUs and consensus-based metadata within individual clusters, enabling a more robust characterization of a cluster’s characteristic community and leading to more reliable statistical analyses and distance calculations (Figures S5K and S5L). However, clusters of all sizes are available for download at www.microbeatlas.org, enabling researchers to apply their own custom filters. A majority rule was used to assign projects, environments, sequencing strategies, hosts IDs, and metadata keywords to community clusters, where terms found in at least 80% of the samples were assigned to the whole cluster. Clusters assigned to project ID ERP012803 were annotated as American Gut Project (AGP), clusters assigned to projects SRA: SRP002395, SRP002860, SRP002163, or SRA: SRP002012 were annotated as Human Microbiome Project (HMP), and clusters assigned to any project from https://ebi-metagenomics.github.io/blog/2019/04/17/Earth-Microbiome-Project/ were annotated as Earth Microbiome Project (EMP). 26,487 community clusters with confidently assigned main environments and sequencing technologies underwent UMAP projection and follow-up analyses (see subsection ‘‘Statistics, normalization, and visualization’’). For environmental annotations in Figure 2A, our 65 keyword-based sub-environments were manually summarized into up to three categories per main environment. For the analysis of human exposed body sites and human gut microbiomes, community clusters assigned to ‘‘Animal (human)’’ were further annotated as exposed body sites if their consensus keywords included "vagina", "oral", "skin", "saliva", "salivary", "human-oral", "urogenital", "mouth", "nare", "nasal", "anterior nare", "lung", "airways", or "nasopharynx". They were instead annotated as gut if their consensus keywords included "gastrointestinal", "feces", "gut", "gastric", "stomach", "gastrointestinal tract", or "human-gut". To reduce noise, human and environmental clusters in Figure S1S were further refined by applying coordinate masks to their core regions within the projection. For the mouse and human gut comparisons, only clusters annotated as ‘‘Animal (human)’’ or ‘‘Animal (mouse)’’ were considered. Core cluster boundaries were refined via hierarchical density clustering with HDBSCAN104 v0.8.40. Keywords enriched in human and mouse, respectively, were computed using Fisher’s exact test (two-tailed) to remove noisy and uninformative terms. Significantly enriched and depleted keywords were then pooled and semantically clustered. This involved i) a system prompt that confined the LLM’s response to an easily parsable format and ii) a user prompt with the clustering instructions. Next, the semantic distance for each pair of keywords was computed as: sij = Sij − Dij Cij Where s is semantic distance between keywords i and j, S represents the number of contexts in which i and j cluster together, D represents the number of contexts in which they cluster apart, and C represents the total number of contexts in which they both appear. Geographic mapping and IUCN annotation Sampling coordinates (latitude and longitude) were parsed directly from dedicated SRA annotation fields; a total of 31 distinct field headers were encountered that either matched ‘longit*’ or ‘latitud*’ separately, or a combination of ‘lat*’ and ‘lon*’. For the coordinates themselves, a variety of different formatting choices were observed, such as decimal vs. degree notations, various levels of precision, different syntaxes, and different ways of distinguishing North/South and East/West. The coordinates were thus parsed using distinct regular expressions in custom Python code; in an iterative procedure, coordinate records that initially failed to parse were inspected manually and corresponding regular expressions for special cases were added (a total of 11 regular expressions were eventually needed).

In a subsequent step, the resulting sample placements were inspected visually on a zoomable world-map; large sample collections with improbable coordinates stood out and were followed up manually. Obvious errors were corrected if possible; these included accidental lat/lon reversals in the original metadata, as well as series of samples that differed systematically by one numerical unit from each other, creating vertical or horizontal lines of samples on the map (these possibly originate from MS Excel manipulation errors).

Diversity estimation and latitudinal analyses Total community richness was extrapolated using formula 7 in 119 (based on an improved version of the Good-Turing frequency estimator): ̂Sn+m∗= Sobs + ̂f0 [ 1 − ( 1 − f1 n̂f0+f1 )m∗] For additional consistency checks, sample richness was also estimated by counting observed OTUs in samples rarefied to 1000 SSU reads. Beta diversity between samples and community clusters was computed as Bray-Curtis distance on relative abundances, using the Distances.jl package108 (v0.10). Weighted and unweighted unifrac dissimilarities were computed using Strided State Unifrac105 from the biocore.unifrac package (v1.3.0). For community clusters, beta diversity between two clusters A and B was defined as the average pairwise Bray-Curtis or unifrac dissimilarity between all samples in A and all samples in B. For unifrac, each community cluster was reduced to three randomly chosen samples to improve computational tractability. The phylogenetic tree used for unifrac was computed by aligning all 16S reference sequences with INFERNAL v1.1.2 (for details, see section ‘‘OTU clustering and taxonomic classification’’ and97), followed by maximum-likelihood tree inference using FastTree124 v2.1.10 (parameters: ‘‘-nt -gtr -gamma’’).

For the latitudinal diversity analysis of marine communities, only samples with sub-environment ‘‘marine’’, ‘‘ocean’’, or ‘‘sea’’ were considered (50,480 samples). The ‘‘Global oceans and seas’’ shapefile125 (version 1) was used to remove samples not located in oceans, resulting in a final set of 43,156 samples. For latitudinal diversity analysis of terrestrial samples, diversity curves were computed per IUCN biome in order to not mix vastly different ecosystems (e.g., agricultural soils and deserts) within one analysis. Only biomes covering at least 3 of 10 latitudinal bins with a minimum of 100 samples per bin were included. These criteria were only matched by "Deserts and semi-deserts" (600 samples, 3 bins), "Temperate-boreal forests and woodlands" (2,464 samples, 4 bins), and "Intensive land use" (42,133 samples, 7 bins).

To compute latitudinal abundance associations, latitudes were discretized into 50 evenly sized bins and 97% OTUs present in at least 25 bins and with minimum global prevalence of 250 were considered. For each OTU, relative abundances per bin were averaged and log10-transformed, followed by computing Pearson’s ρ between log-transformed averages and per-bin latitudes. Each OTU was assigned to a main environment based on its maximum average abundance within the four major environments: animal, aquatic, soil, and plant.

To explore the effect of latitudinal biases, a conservative downsampling of our latitude bins was performed. This resulted in equal sample counts per bin, both for per-OTU latitudinal associations and for marine and terrestrial diversity gradients. For per-OTU latitudinal associations, all 50 latitudinal bins for each OTU were randomly downsampled to 10 samples per bin (dropping bins with <10 samples), followed by re-computation of latitudinal associations per environment. This loss of data resulted in a sharp drop of significant associations (9321 associations, down from 37,393), but yielded 91.1% (down from 93.3%) negative associations, in line with the results on the full data set. Environmental trends were also comparable: 49% associations were aquatic (down from 54%), 39% animal (up from 31%), 12% soil (down from 14%), and 1% plant (down from 2%). Soil associations appeared slightly more balanced in the downsampled dataset (47% equatorial associations, down from 63%), while other environments show slightly increased equatorial associations (95%-100%, up from 86%-99%). These per-OTU environmental abundance profiles were then converted into proportions to make profiles of abundant and rare OTUs comparable. QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS Latitudinal diversity modeling Separate Generalized Linear Models were fitted (using GLM.jl107 v1.9) (Figures S4P–S4R) to 5130 marine samples for which latitudes, temperatures (in Celsius) and physical sampling depths (in meters) were available, and to 14,162 terrestrial samples for which latitudes and sampling depths were available. To reduce noise, latitudes were binned (bin width: 15◦) prior to model fitting. Low-diversity sponge and sediment samples (mainly from two studies: SRA: ERP013416, ERP020690) locally distorted diversity estimates (Figures S4U and S4V), samples with keywords ‘‘sediment’’ or ‘‘sponge’’ were therefore removed for this comparison. Estimated diversity values were log10-transformed prior to model fitting in order to normalize residual distributions, and all predictor variables were log- and zscore-transformed to normalize variable ranges. To handle zeros, temperatures were converted to Kelvin and depth was increased by a pseudo-count of 1e-9. The fitting procedure was weighted by the number of samples per bin (parameter ‘‘wts’’ in function ‘‘fit()’’). For each dataset, individual (latitude, depth, temperature; where available) and combined (latitude + temperature, latitude + depth) models were fitted with only linear or linear + quadratic terms (formulas: log10(OTUs) ∼var (linear) and log10(OTUs) ∼var + var2 (quadratic), where var = latitude, temperature, or sampling depth). General statistics and normalization Unless otherwise stated, relative OTU abundances were used for analyses: per-sample OTU abundances were normalized to a range of 0 to 1 by dividing by the total number of mapped reads. Unless otherwise stated, logarithms throughout the paper were computed by adding a pseudo-count equal to the minimum non-zero value divided by 10 to all values. Statistical tests and effect sizes throughout the manuscript were computed using HypothesisTests.jl106 v0.11 and scipy103 v1.10. In order to account for environmental sampling biases, in particular an overrepresentation of animal samples, we avoided aggregated analysis across environments where possible, and instead computed adequate statistics per environment, followed by comparative analyses. In addition, we utilized simple statistics (e.g., averages of: numbers of OTUs, relative abundances) that can be more robustly estimated across environments with varying sample sizes. Finally, the usage of community clusters in many of our analyses can help reduce sampling biases by grouping redundantly sequenced community samples together.
