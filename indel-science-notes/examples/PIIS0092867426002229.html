SUMMARY Aging is a major risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases, yet the underlying epigenetic mechanisms remain unclear. Here, we generated a comprehensive single-nucleus cell atlas of brain aging across multiple brain regions, comprising 132,551 single-cell methylomes and 72,666 joint chromatin conformation-methyl­ ome nuclei. Integration with companion transcriptomic and chromatin accessibility data yielded a cross-mo­ dality taxonomy of 36 major cell types. We observed that transposable element (TE) methylation alone distin­ guished age groups, showing cell-type-specific genome-wide demethylation. Chromatin conformation analysis demonstrated age-related increases in topologically associated domain (TAD) boundary strength with enhanced accessibility at CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) binding sites. Spatial transcriptomics across 895,296 cells revealed regional heterogeneity during aging within identical cell types. Finally, we developed deep-learning models that reliably predict age-related gene expression changes using multi-modal epige­ netic features, providing mechanistic insights into gene regulation. Age-related comparisons use a 2-month baseline reflecting the late-adolescent/early-young adult stage. This dataset advances our under­ standing of brain aging and offers potential translational applications.

INTRODUCTION

Aging is the primary risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, Huntington’s disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).1 Age-related brain changes, particularly in regions critical for attention, mem­ ory, emotion, and motor functions, severely impact life quality.1 Understanding how aging contributes to the progression of these diseases is essential for implementing preventive strate­ gies and developing targeted therapies.

Hallmarks of aging include inflammation, genome instability, and epigenetic change.2 Epigenetic alterations play an important role in mammalian aging,3,4 and partial reprogramming can reset these changes and extend lifespan.5–7 Among the epigenetic modifications, cytosine DNA methylation (5mC), a covalent genome modification in post-mitotic cells throughout their life­ span, is associated with neuronal function, behavior, and various diseases. In addition to 5mC that occurs at CpG sites (mCG), non-CpG cytosine methylation (mCH) is also prevalent in neurons.11,12 Together, CpG and CpH methylation modulate transcription factor (TF) binding and gene transcription through their dynamic occurrence at regulato­ ry elements and gene bodies. The local depletion of DNA methyl­ ation is a reliable marker of active regulatory elements such as enhancers.

DNA methylation is critical for genome stability maintenance, largely by silencing transposable elements (TEs). TEs are mobile genetic sequences comprising a substantial fraction of mamma­ lian genomes, including retrotransposons (long interspersed nu­ clear elements [LINEs], short interspersed nuclear elements [SINEs], and long terminal repeat [LTR] elements), which mobi­ lize through an RNA intermediate, and DNA transposons, which move as DNA. Although TE activity is normally suppressed through diverse epigenetic mechanisms, this control becomes compromised during aging, leading to TE reactivation.13 Such activation can cause double-stranded DNA breaks14 and trigger inflammatory responses,15–20 ultimately contributing to cellular dysfunction and age-related decline.19,21,22 Beyond DNA methylation, cis-regulatory elements act over long distances to regulate target genes. Disruption of genome stability and 3D organization is implicated in neurodegeneration and aging,23–25 but how contact frequency between enhancers and promoters relates to epigenetic and transcriptomic changes during brain aging remains unclear.

Recent studies have characterized cell-type-specific tran­ scriptomic26 and selected epigenetic changes during brain ag­ ing,24,27–29 but a comprehensive multi-omic analysis across mul­ tiple brain regions has been lacking. To address this gap and enable mechanistic insight into region- and cell-type-spe­ cific aging effects, we generated an extensive single-cell dataset spanning DNA methylation, chromatin conformation, and spatial transcriptomics, complemented by a companion study30 providing chromatin accessibility and transcriptome data across eight neurodegeneration-vulnerable regions.31–34 Using our single-cell whole-genome methylation data, we show that neuronal age-hypomethylated (age-hypo) regions are enriched for activator protein 1 (AP-1) motifs, whereas glial age-hypermethylated (age-hyper) regions are enriched for Sox family factors. Notably, methylation patterns in TE regions can distinguish both cell types and age groups. Cell-type-specific TE demethylation is accompanied by increased chromatin accessibility and transcription, highlighting cell types most affected by TE reactivation.

Single-cell chromatin conformation data reveal a progressive strengthening of topologically associated domain (TAD) bound­ aries with increased chromatin accessibility at CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) sites, suggesting broad structural remodeling dur­ ing aging. Spatial transcriptomics further uncovers region-spe­ cific variability in aging responses within the same cell types. We also develop deep-learning models that reliably predict age-related gene expression changes from epigenetic features. Together, these findings provide a comprehensive resource for cellular, spatial, and regulatory analysis of brain aging and a framework for future ‘‘virtual’’ aging models.

RESULTS

Integrative single-cell multi-omic dataset of aging mouse brain We refined and employed snmC-seq3,35,36 an advanced single- nucleus methylome sequencing technique, to achieve base-res­ olution profiling of genome-wide 5-methylcytosine (5mC) across eight brain regions in C57BL/6 male mice (Figure 1A). These ma­ jor regions, which include the anterior hippocampus (AHC), pos­ terior hippocampus (PHC), frontal cortex (FC), amygdala (AMY), nucleus accumbens (NACB), periaqueductal gray/pontine cen­ tral gray (PAG/PCG), entorhinal cortex (ENT), and caudate puta­ men (CP) (Figure 1B) (brain dissection details; see Table S1), were meticulously dissected from male mice at 2, 9, and 18 months of age (Figure 1A). In this study, we used 2-month- old mice as the young baseline, consistent with multiple brain atlas projects36,38,39 and prior aging studies.26,40,41 However, 2 months corresponds to a late-adolescent/early-young adult stage rather than a fully mature adult. This distinction should be considered when interpreting the aging trajectories. Each re­ gion was sampled in two replicates, pooled from 2 to 7 different animals per replicate (STAR Methods). We incorporated 51,141 nuclei from 2-month-old male mice previously generated by the Brain Initiative Cell Census Network (BICCN)36 using snmC-seq3 (Table S2). To ensure compatibility, we generated an additional 9,510 snmC-seq3 nuclei from 2-month-old male mice (FC and ENT) to evaluate potential batch effects. This anal­ ysis confirmed minimal batch effects, with a strong uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) overlap (align­ ment score42: 0.86 ± 0.07) (Figures S1A and S1B) and substan­ tially fewer batch-related differentially methylated regions (DMRs) than age-related DMRs (described in later sections) (Figure S1C). Additionally, we employed snm3C-seq43 to assess the DNA methylome and chromatin conformation simultaneously in the same brain cell from the same regions of female mice (Figure 1A). This approach integrates the 3D genome context across various cell types and ages, enriching our understanding of cellular changes over time.

Single nuclei were captured by neuronal nuclei (NeuN)-based fluorescence-activated nuclei sorting (FANS), enriching for neu­ rons (STAR Methods). Collectively, we obtained 132,551 DNA methylome profiles that passed quality control (QC) (STAR Methods). The snmC-seq3 data averaged 1.07 ± 0.5 million final reads (mean ± SD) per profile (Table S3). We also obtained 72,666 joint methylome and 3C profiles that passed QC, with each cell from this dataset having 1.9 ± 0.5 million final reads (Figure S1D). The 3C profiles revealed 442,000 ± 156,000 cis- long-range (two anchors >2,500 bp apart) and 357,000 ± 138,000 trans contacts per cell, respectively (Figure S1E; Table S4).

After QC and preprocessing, we clustered and integrated the snmC and snm3C datasets (STAR Methods). Utilizing the anno­ tations from BICCN,36 we identified 85 neuronal and 10 non- neuronal cell types (Tables S3 and S4). We focused on 36 major cell types, each represented by over 100 cells in every age group across both datasets, ensuring sufficient coverage for down­ stream analysis (Figure 1C). To systematically identify alterations in gene expression and annotate the accessibility status of age-DMRs, we integrated our methylome data with our companion datasets.30 These included single-nucleus assay for transposase-accessible chro­ matin using sequencing (snATAC-seq) and 10× Multiome data (Table S2) generated from the same dissected samples used in our data. We adopted our previously modified Seurat frame­ work36 to integrate these datasets (STAR Methods), which effec­ tively matched neuronal spatial distribution (Figure 1D), clusters (Figure S2D), and cell types (Figure S2E). This integration gener­ ated cell-type-specific pseudo-bulk profiles spanning five mo­ lecular modalities: mCH/mCG fraction, chromatin conformation, chromatin accessibility, and gene expression.

To achieve higher spatial resolution, we employed multiplexed error-robust fluorescence in situ hybridization (MERFISH)45 technology for in situ profiling of gene expression in brain slices. We designed a 500-gene panel (Table S5) comprising 233 cell- type markers, along with 267 aging-related genes selected based on differential methylation analyses, Alzheimer’s disease associations, senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) markers, immune cell identity, etc. (STAR Methods).

We profiled seven coronal sections from mice aged 2, 9, and 18 months. These slices share the brain regions included in our snmC and snm3C datasets (Figure S3A). After QC (Figure S3B; STAR Methods), we obtained 895,296 MERFISH cells and as­ signed cell types through integration with the whole-brain sin­ gle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)39 dataset (Figures S3C– S3F; Table S6; STAR Methods).

To illustrate the power of this multi-omic aging dataset, we use Il33, an inflammation gene upregulated in aging oligodendro­ cytes (Oligo NN),40 as an example. Beyond confirming increased Il33 RNA expression, we identify genome regions showing age- related loss of methylation and increased chromatin accessibility as putative cis-regulatory elements (Figure 1E). The 3C heatmap shows higher contact frequency, suggesting increased enhancer-promoter interactions (Figure 1F). MERFISH spatial analysis further reveals Il33 upregulation primarily concentrated in white matter and subcortical areas (Figure 1G). By integrating single-cell methylation, chromatin conformation, and spatial transcriptomics, our study builds a multi-modality atlas of the ag­ ing mouse brain across regions and cell types. This resource supports detailed analyses and yields unique biological insights, described in the following sections.

Age-related differential methylation signals in neurons and non-neurons We show that the genome-wide average methylation remains largely stable across cell types (Figures S4A and S4B), replicates (Figures S4C and S4D), and sexes during aging, consistent with previous studies.46 Comparing 18-month-old to 2-month-old mice, we observed only subtle global mCG change, with mCG fraction shifting by 0.008 ± 0.005 in males and 0.006 ± 0.006 in females (Figure S4B). Despite the overall stability, we identified age-related differentially methylated genes (DMGs) and DMRs at cell-type resolution. We noted that some 2- to 9-month methylation and chromatin changes could include contributions from late developmental maturation, as 2-month-old mice are not yet fully mature adults. Because the age-differential signals we report proceed monotonically from 2 to 18 months, these sig­ nals nevertheless reflect progressive aging-related processes, but this developmental component should be acknowledged.

For DMG analysis, we classified all genes passing QC in the 10× Multiome data into three groups: (1) DMGs, regardless of differentially expressed gene (DEG) status; (2) DMGs that are also DEGs (DMG and DEG); and (3) DEGs only (DEGs not DMGs) (STAR Methods). Gene-body mCH and mCG alterations exhibited strong negative correlations with mRNA expression across most cell types, particularly for DMG and DEG (Figures S5A and S5B). For example, ‘‘L6 IT CTX Glut’’ showed a Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) of −0.49 (false discovery rate [FDR] < 10−30), and ‘‘Oligo NN’’ cells showed a PCC of −0.58 (FDR < 10−30) (Figure S5B). Notably, hypermethylated (aged > young) CG-DMGs in males were enriched in neuron dif­ ferentiation pathways, suggesting potential dysregulation of neuronal function during aging (Figure S5C). Interestingly, female striatum (STR) inhibitory neurons have the most abundant changes on X chromosome genes but not in males. These genes show decreased mCH methylation with aging (Figure S5D) and increased gene expression (Figure S5E). For example, the Dmd gene encodes the inhibitory synapse scaffold protein dystro­ phin47 and is linked to intellectual disability.48,49 This aligns with previous observations in the female mouse hippocam­ pus (HC).50 DMRs serve as predictors of cis-regulatory elements, with hy­ pomethylation typically indicating active regulatory status. We identified age-DMRs separately in males and females within each major cell type across age groups, categorizing them into three classes: female specific, male specific, and sex shared (STAR Methods). On average, we identified 8,284 ± 13,894 sex-shared DMRs, 9,498 ± 12,515 male-specific DMRs, and 7,325 ± 3,155 female-specific DMRs across major cell types (Figure S6A). Male-specific DMRs exhibited substantially greater mCG changes in males than in females, while female-specific DMRs showed correspondingly larger mCG changes in females than males (Figures S6B and S6C). In contrast, sex-shared DMRs displayed comparable and significant mCG changes in both sexes (Figures S6B and S6C), with mCG fraction changes highly correlated between males and females across all cell types (PCC: 0.77 ± 0.09) (Figure S6D), validating our categoriza­ tion strategy.

Here, we mainly focus our analysis on sex-shared age-DMRs as they are the most robust signals. We identified more numerous and longer age-DMRs in non-neurons than in neu­ rons, independent of cell numbers (Figures 2A and S6E). Among these, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) displayed the most abundant methylation changes. These findings align with previous observations26,40 for age-related transcriptome alter­ ations, suggesting that non-neuronal cells are more substantially affected during aging. Specifically, we found 28,950 ± 29,911 age-DMRs among non-neurons and 4,672 ± 3,724 among neu­ rons. Here, we define DMRs that progressively lose methylation with age as age-hypo DMRs (2 > 9 > 18 months), while those that continuously gain methylation are age-hyper DMRs (2 < 9 < 18 months). Neurons exhibited a higher proportion of intragenic age-DMRs compared with non-neuronal cells, and this pattern was consistent across age-hypo and age- hyper DMR categories (Figure S6F). Notably, 88% of glutamater­ gic excitatory neurons exhibit a higher proportion of age-hypo DMRs (Figure 2C). Both age-hypo and age-hyper DMRs show a strong negative correlation with chromatin accessibility status across ages (FDR < 0.001) in most cell types (Figures S6G and S6H), e.g., r = −0.8, FDR < 0.001 for ‘‘DG Glut,’’ granule cells located in the dentate gyrus (DG) (Figures S6H and S6I).

To explore the potential regulatory roles of DNA methylation changes, we performed sequence motif enrichment analysis (STAR Methods) to identify TFs that may bind at age-DMRs.51 TF binding sites are mostly repressed if they gain DNA methyl­ ation.52 We observed significant enrichment of AP-1 family mo­ tifs (for example, Fos and Jun) in neuronal age-hypo DMRs (Figure 2D), consistent with observations in human cortical and hippocampal neurons.29,53 Activation of AP-1 leads to chromatin opening, which disrupts neuron cell identity54 and has been linked to inflammaging in aged cells.53,55 For age-hyper DMRs, motifs of the Sox TF family, which is known for promoting self- renewal and differentiation,56 are enriched in glial cells, including astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and OPCs (Figure 2E). Given that Sox10 is particularly crucial for OPC survival and development,57 these results suggest a potential regulatory mechanism contrib­ uting to age-related decline in the OPC population53 and their reduced differentiation potential41 (Figure S6J). Our companion paper30 discusses the age-related dysregulation of TFs in greater detail.

We also performed motif enrichment in male- and female-spe­ cific age-DMRs. For age-hypo DMRs, both male- and female- specific sets showed significant enrichment for AP-1 family mo­ tifs (Figures S7A and S7B). For age-hyper DMRs, we observed enrichment of Lhx and Dlx family motifs in both sexes (Figures S7C and S7D). Notably, the nuclear respiratory factor (NRF) family motifs were significantly enriched in female age-hy­ per DMRs specifically within cortical cell types (Figure S7D), a pattern absent in sex-shared or male-specific DMRs. NRF1 reg­ ulates mitochondrial function and energy metabolism and is significantly reduced before cognitive decline.58 Prior studies have demonstrated female-specific deficiency in NRF1-driven mitochondrial biogenesis in cerebellar granule neurons under oxidative stress.59 Our results suggest that female-specific NRF hypermethylation may contribute to age-related cortical vulnerability.

Retrotransposons emerge as methylation aging hotspots We next investigated whether certain genomic regions are particularly enriched with aging signals. Methylation aging hot­ spots were identified as non-overlapping 500-bp regions con­ taining more than 10 significant age-related differentially methyl­ ated sites (age-DMSs) across cell types (Figures 2F and S7E; STAR Methods). The number of age-DMSs is not correlated with CpG density (Figure S7F). We identified 3,519 age-hypo and 969 age-hyper hotspots (Figures 2G and S7G). Most hot­ spots are shared among cell types (85.1% of age-hyper; 64.9% of age-hypo hotspots), whereas some represent ‘‘cell- type-unique hotspots,’’ characterized by dense signals (>80%) from a single cell type (Figure S7H). We observed that 73.7% of age-hypo hotspots and 54.9% of age-hyper hotspots showed overlap with genes, which were subsequently termed ‘‘hotspot genes.’’ Interestingly, age-hypo hotspot genes show significant enrichment in GTPase-related processes, suggesting that crit­ ical neuronal functions, including synaptic plasticity60 and auto­ phagy61 (Figure S7I), might have been impacted. Surprisingly, the hotspot region with the most abundant hypomethylated sig­ nals was located on chromosome 13. This region, along with three additional proximal regions on chromosome 13, was found within gene deserts and overlapped with retrotransposons, spe­ cifically the LTR class, ERV1 family, and murian retrovirus- related sequences (MuRRS-int) subfamily (LTR-ERV1-MuRRS- int) (Figure 2G). We statistically checked the enrichment of TE subfamilies in age-hypo hotspot regions, highlighting a 13-fold enrichment in LTR-ERV1-MuRRS-int (Figure 2H; STAR Methods).

TE methylation level distinguishes cell types and age groups DNA cytosine methylation represents a primary epigenetic modification that maintains transcriptional suppression of retro­ transposons.13,14,62 We initially focused on LINE regions, which are the most abundant retrotransposon class in humans.

LINE1 elements are known to perform autonomous transposition in humans and contribute to age-related neurological disor­ ders.63 Our analysis revealed that intergenic and intragenic LINE regions function as effective features for clustering based on mCG methylation levels, demonstrating clear separation be­ tween cell types (Figures 3A and S8A; STAR Methods). This sug­ gests that even intergenic TE methylation is critical for cellular identity, independent of gene-body methylation levels. Interest­ ingly, we also observed pronounced segregation of age groups (Figure 3A). The DG Glut exemplifies this age-related pattern most clearly, characterized by an increased ratio of hypomethy­ lated LINE regions in aged neurons (Figure 3B) and the lowest alignment score42 between ages (Figure 3C). Surprisingly, when DG Glut cells were clustered separately, age-related sep­ aration appeared exclusively in cells from AHC (Figure S8B), revealing regional heterogeneity—a phenomenon explored in subsequent sections. Beyond DG Glut, other cell types with age separations also exhibited an increased ratio of hypomethy­ lated TE regions (Figure S8C). These patterns were not unique to LINE elements, with similar methylation dynamics observed in LTRs, SINEs, and DNA transposon regions (Figures S8D–S8F).

Increase in compartment strength during aging Our datasets enabled us to comprehensively investigate cell- type-specific 3D genome dynamics during aging. We focused on systematic assessments of variability in chromatin compart­ ments, TADs, and loops. Our analysis reveals slight increases in short-to-long-range contact ratios and cis-to-trans contact ra­ tios when comparing samples from 18-month-old mice with those from 2-month-old mice, consistent with observations in human hippocampal neurons53 (Figures S10A–S10C).

We expanded our analysis to the chromatin compartment, a crucial component of genome topology that organizes genomic regions spanning tens to hundreds of megabases.69 The genome is organized into two primary compartments: A (active chromatin) and B (silent chromatin), reflecting distinct functional states.69 Our study identified a total of 897 cell-type-specific age-related differential compartments (age-DCs), with 528 (58.9%) exhibiting A-to-B compartment switches (Figures S10D and S10E; STAR Methods). These findings suggest substantial chromatin reorga­ nization during aging, potentially impacting gene expression. For instance, chromatin rearrangements were observed on chromo­ some 2: 168.3–173.8 Mb in oligodendrocytes. This region dis­ plays a switch from A to B compartments during aging, accom­ panied by reduced chromatin interaction frequencies (Figure S10F). This reorganization correlated with significant downregu­ lation of Bcas1 (Figure S10G), a gene essential for early-myelinat­ ing oligodendrocytes.70 Notably, most excitatory neurons ex­ hibited a higher ratio of B-to-A compartment switches (Figure S10H), similar to prior observations of heterochromatin loss in excitatory neurons.28 We quantified compartment strength using the formula (AA + BB)/(AB + BA) and observed a consistent in­ crease across cell types (Figures S10I–S10K; STAR Methods), in line with recent findings in cerebellar granular cells.24 Specif­ ically, excitatory neurons show an increase in B-B compartment interactions, while inhibitory neurons demonstrate more A-A compartment contacts (Figure S10K). These results underscore both similarities and differences in compartment alterations across different cell types.

Increase in TAD boundary probabilities and formation of smaller TADs TADs are highly self-interacting chromosomal regions bounded by CTCF protein binding sites, which create insulated neighborhoods essential for gene regulation.71 Together with cohesin, CTCF es­ tablishes chromatin loops that define these domain boundaries and regulate enhancer-promoter interactions.72 Using contact matrices imputed at 25 kb resolution, we identified TADs in single cells (STAR Methods) and discovered a significant increase in the number of domains accompanied by a reduction in their average size during aging across cell types (FDR < 10−10) (Figures 4A and S11A). To explore the reason for this, we analyzed age-related dif­ ferential TAD boundaries (age-DBs) using data on boundary prob­ ability (STAR Methods), which means the proportion of cells where a genomic bin serves as a domain boundary. We identified a total of 2,469 cell-type-specific age-DBs showing significant variability (FDR < 1 × 10−3; STAR Methods). Notably, 80.8% (1,994/2,469) of age-DBs were found in excitatory neurons, suggesting their partic­ ular susceptibility to TAD structural changes (Figure 4B). Remark­ ably, 93.7% of these age-DBs exhibited increased boundary prob­ abilities along with decreased insulation scores at the pseudo-bulk level (Figure 4C), a trend that progresses continuously with aging (Figure S11B).

Regional variability of aging effects at cell-type resolution The mouse brain has tens of millions of cells organized into intri­ cate anatomical structures, each with unique cellular composi­ tions and neural connectivity patterns. Previous studies have shown that cells of the same type exhibit DNA methylation het­ erogeneity across different brain regions.36,44,79 However, our understanding of how the same cell type undergoes aging across different brain regions remains poorly understood. To address this gap, we leveraged our single-cell, multi-region ag­ ing dataset to conduct age-differential analysis, stratifying cells by cell type, region, and age, requiring ≥100 cells per cell type-region-age group. Our findings revealed notable regional variability in aging-related changes at the cell-type level. Specif­ ically, we systematically quantified the number of age-DMRs (Figure S12A), age-DEGs (Figure S12B), CG-DMGs (Figure S12C), age-DBs (Figure S12D), and age-related differential loops (age-DLs) (Figure S12E).

To further illustrate regional heterogeneity in aging-related changes, we focused on DG Glut, which processes sensory in­ formation critical for memory formation80,81 and also plays a role in depression.82,83 DG neurons exhibit distinct connectivity patterns along the anterior-to-posterior axis.84 In our dissection experiments (Table S1; STAR Methods), the AHC corresponds to the dorsal HC, while the PHC includes the ventral regions plus part of the dorsal HC. The dorsal HC mediates cognitive functions, whereas the ventral regions are involved in regulating stress, emotion, and affect.85 In our analysis, clustering of DG Glut cells by LINE regions revealed age-related separations that were exclusive to the AHC, not the PHC, suggesting stron­ ger LINE activation in anterior DG Glut (Figure S8B). Further dif­ ferential analysis indicated that age-associated alterations were more pronounced in the AHC, with a higher abundance of age- DMRs, age-DEGs, age-DMGs, age-DBs, and age-DLs (Figure 5A).

Gene Ontology enrichment of AHC DG Glut age-DEGs identi­ fied in our companion study30 showed significant enrichment in axonogenesis and neuronal projection pathways (Figure 5B), suggesting that aging may preferentially affect neuronal projec­ tions in anterior DG Glut neurons. We also compared gene expression of DG Glut in AHC and PHC using our annotated MERFISH dataset (Figure S3). Notably, the immunoglobulin heavy-chain gene Ighm was upregulated in anterior DG Glut (Figure 5C) but not in the PHC regions (Figure 5D). This increased expression correlated with enhanced chromatin interactions observed solely in the AHC (Figures 5C and 5D), where upstream regulatory regions also showed DNA hypomethylation and increased chromatin accessibility (Figure 5E). Age-related Ighm upregulation has also been observed in cortical excitatory neu­ rons showing specific spatial patterns.40 This upregulation of Ighm in anterior DG neurons may indicate heightened inflamma­ tory stress in this neuronal population during aging.

Unlike neurons with their distinct anatomical organization, non-neurons are distributed throughout the brain.36,39,86 Using anterior-to-posterior coronal slices from our MERFISH dataset (Figure S3), we performed slice-specific age-DEGs analysis (STAR Methods). We first examined oligodendrocytes, which provide myelination to protect neuronal axons.87 These cells ex­ hibited marked regional variation in age-DEGs across brain re­ gions. Surprisingly, the C4b gene, a complement component and major schizophrenia risk factor,88 previously reported with increased expression in aged mice89 and neurodegeneration models,90 showed selective upregulation in posterior brain slices (Figures 5F and 5G). Spatial mapping showed that C4b upregu­ lation was most pronounced in subcortical regions and white matter of the corpus callosum (Figure 5G). Our analysis also re­ vealed extensive regional heterogeneity in aging across other non-neuronal cells. For instance, the Nlrp3 gene, a component of the inflammasome that detects cellular damage and initiates inflammation,91 showed significant upregulation specifically in posterior microglia (Figure S12F). Similarly, Irf1, implicated in cytokine interferon production,92 was upregulated in astrocytes and OPCs exclusively in the posterior slices (Figure S12F). These findings clearly demonstrate that both neuronal and non- neuronal cells display region-specific aging signatures even within the same cell type.

The model assigns the highest attention weight (0.75) to the ATAC peak, and genome browser views indicate that expression changes are predomi­ nantly driven by altered chromatin accessibility (Figure 6F). Similarly, Ppfibp2, which regulates cell adhesion,95 exhibits age upregulation in Oligo NN, largely attributable to gene-body age-DMRs, as reflected by an attention weight of 0.54 and enrichment of age-hypo DMRs (Figure S13E). Another example is Akt3, a kinase critical for neuronal survival,96 associated with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder.97,98 In "L6 CT CTX Glut,’’ the model assigns a high weight (0.43) to the 3C loop, consistent with increased chromatin interactions (Figure 6G). Together, these examples demonstrate that the EAT model’s self-attention module effectively quantifies the contributions of distinct epige­ netic features to gene expression changes.

To systematically characterize these cell-type-specific regula­ tions, we calculated the average attention weight for each epige­ netic feature across gene categories (Figure 6H). Notably, mCH gene-body weights were predominantly allocated to neuronal cells, with neurons exhibiting higher average attention weights (17.6% ± 11.1%) compared with non-neuronal cells (7.6% ± 3.6%) (Figure 6H). This observation aligns with previous studies indicating that non-CpG cytosines are abundantly methylated uniquely in mouse and human neurons during development.12,99 Further, our analysis uncovered gene-body ATAC peaks that were more strongly associated with age-related gene upregula­ tion (average attention weight: 20.2% ± 5.9%) than with downre­ gulation (16.8% ± 5.3%). This finding can be further supported by the fact that age-up DEGs demonstrate significantly (p < 0.01) higher correlation between ATAC peaks and RNA sig­ nals than downregulated DEGs (Figure 6I). Specifically, upregu­ lated genes exhibit an average PCC of 0.24 ± 0.2, whereas downregulated genes show a substantially lower correlation of 0.03 ± 0.21 (Figure 6I). Conversely, changes in enhancers had a greater impact on gene downregulation during aging (15.2% ± 7.5%) compared with upregulation (11.9% ± 6.5%) (Figure 6H). Collectively, our study establishes a comprehensive and interpretable machine-learning framework for understand­ ing age-related transcriptomic changes through the integration of multiple epigenetic modalities and features.

DISCUSSION

Our single-cell multi-omic data reveal fundamental aspects of epigenetic aging in the mouse brain, highlighting the vulnerability of non-neuronal populations, cell-type-specific TE derepression, and increased TAD boundary strength. These findings under­ score aging as a cell-type- and region-specific process.

Both transcriptomic and methylomic data point to glial cells as especially susceptible during aging.26,40 Motif analysis reveals AP-1 activation, consistent with reports in hematopoietic stem cells,100 kidney, and liver,55 suggesting a conserved role for AP-1 in aging, which may disrupt cell identity.54 Notably, increased methylation at Sox motifs in OPCs suggests a mechanism by which OPC differentiation and myelin integrity could be impaired.57 TE methylation signatures reliably encode cell identity and ag­ ing status. We observe coordinated TE hypomethylation, increased chromatin accessibility, and transcription, consistent with age-related TE activation. L1 overexpression has been shown to exacerbate chromatin instability22 and innate immune activation,19,21 whereas clearance of LINE1 cDNA reduces inflammation and DNA damage and extends lifespan. By distin­ guishing potentially pathogenic from regulatory TE activity, we provide a framework for targeted TE-silencing interventions. We present the most comprehensive single-cell chromatin conformation dataset on the aging brain to date. A substantial fraction of TAD boundaries gain strength, accompanied by increased CTCF accessibility, paralleling patterns seen in human DG granule cells.53 These strengthened boundaries may restrict promoter-enhancer interactions, contributing to downregulation of genes reliant on cross-boundary enhancers.

Our multi-region analysis further highlights spatial heterogene­ ity in brain aging. Ighm is exclusively upregulated in AHC DG Glut; Ighm translation is demonstrated in neurons101 and may function as an adhesion molecule102 or activate the complement system, contributing to brain immune responses.103,104 Non- neurons also exhibit region-specific vulnerability, including white matter susceptibility40,105 and anterior-to-posterior differences. Development of the EAT model highlights the value of integra­ tive approaches for deciphering brain aging. In neurons, mCH carries greater predictive weight than in non-neurons, reflecting its neuron-enriched accumulation.12,36 In contrast, mCG is more influential in non-neurons. Specifically, enhancer mCG is partic­ ularly important for age-up DEGs, while gene-body age-DMRs and average mCG are more relevant to age-down regulation. 3C loop features are more informative in neurons, likely reflecting their shorter-range looping organization.106 Furthermore, chro­ matin accessibility contributes primarily to gene upregulation, suggesting that age-upregulated genes require chromatin open­ ing, whereas downregulated genes may already reside in consti­ tutively open chromatin regions and are regulated through alter­ native mechanisms.

In conclusion, we present a comprehensive single-cell multi- omic atlas of the aging mouse brain. Coupled with interpretable modeling, these data lay the groundwork for mechanistic studies, for targeted treatment strategies, and enable cross-spe­ cies ‘‘virtual’’ models of human brain aging.

Limitations of the study First, 2-month-old mice are used as the young baseline, and although this age is aligned with BICCN and widely used in epi­ genomics studies, it represents a late-adolescent/early-young adult stage rather than full adult maturity. Thus, age-related changes from 2 to 9 months may reflect developmental signals. Second, pooled replicates included varying numbers of animals (2–7), potentially introducing modest variance differences across ages. Though nuclei-level analyses help mitigate this effect, these factors should be considered when interpreting compara­ tive results. METHOD DETAILS Brain tissues collection Brain extraction, slicing, and dissection were performed in an ice-cold dissection buffer as previously described.44 For both snmC- seq3 and snm3C-seq samples, we prepared coronal brain slices at 600 μm intervals spanning the entire brain, producing 18 slices total. Brain regions were dissected according to the Allen Brain Reference Atlas Common Coordinate Framework version 3 (CCFv3).108 Each region was collected from 2-7 animals and pooled to create 2-3 biological replicates for nuclei isolation (Table S7). Because brain region sizes vary, the number of animals contributing to each pooled replicate differed (2-7 animals). While this strategy is standard in large-scale brain single-cell profiling studies, unequal pooling could introduce subtle differences in sta­ tistical power across groups and therefore represents a methodological limitation. Sample size was determined empirically rather than through statistical methods. We established that two to three biological replicates for all single-cell epigenomic experiments would provide minimum reproducibility standards for this large-scale project. We did not implement blinding or randomization during tissue sample handling. All dissected regions underwent digital registration into CCFv3 using ITK-SNAP109 (v4.0.0) at 25 μm resolution.

Nuclei isolation and Sorting Single nuclei were captured using fluorescence-activated nuclei sorting (FANS), with a BD Influx sorter in single-cell mode (1 drop single) to separate neuronal from non-neuronal populations at a ratio of 90:10 (NeuN+:NeuN-), following established protocols as previously described.44 Nuclei were sorted into 384-well plates according to previously described methods.44 NeuN+ neurons constituted 91.6% of snmC and 68.5% of snm3C data, with the remaining data being NeuN- neurons or non-neurons. The snm3C-seq protocol incorporated additional chromatin conformation capture steps during nuclei preparation to enable assessment of chromatin architecture. These steps utilized the Arima-3C BETA Kit (Arima Genomics), with comprehensive methodology detailed in our previous publication.36 Library preparation and Illumina sequencing Both snmC-seq3 and snm3C-seq samples underwent identical library preparation procedures as detailed in our previous publica­ tion.36 We implemented automated workflows using Beckman Biomek i7 and Tecan Freedom Evo platforms to accommodate high- throughput processing requirements. Data integration and annotation We established a highly efficient framework based on the Seurat R package42 integration algorithm to perform atlas-level data inte­ gration with millions of cells. The integration framework consisted of three major steps to align two or more data sets onto the same space: (1) using dimension reduction to derive embedding of the datasets in the same space; (2) using Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) to capture the shared variance across cells between datasets and find anchors as mutual nearest neighbours between the two datasets; (3) aligning the low-dimensional representation of the datasets together with the anchors. Integration and annotation of snmC-seq and snm3C-seq datasets For integration of the snmC-seq and snm3C-seq datasets, we utilized non-overlapping 100-kb chromosome bins as features. Our integration approach began with the selection of cluster-enriched features (CEFs)129 from both mC and m3C cells independently, based on their respective Leiden cluster assignments following methylome-based clustering described in previous sections. The CEF selection was implemented through the cluster_enriched_features function in the ALLCools package. For subsequent integra­ tion, we selected only CEFs that overlapped between the two datasets. We then constructed a principal component analysis (PCA) model using the mC cells and applied this model to transform the m3C cells. To prevent the embedding from being dominated by the initial principal components, we normalized each PC by its corresponding singular value. To find anchors between mC and m3C cells, we first standardized the mC and m3C matrices of CEFs across cells using Z-score scaling. The resulting matrices were designated as X (mC:cell-by-CEF) and Yc(m3C:cell-by-CEF). We applied CCA to identify low- dimensional embeddings that captured shared variation between the two datasets. This was computed through single value decom­ position of their dot products, expressed as USVT = XYT. We normalized the U and V matrices by dividing each row by its L2-norm. These normalized matrices were then used to identify five mutual nearest neighbors as anchors and to score these anchors using methodology consistent with the Seurat approach. To manage computational demands, we randomly selected 100,000 cells from each dataset as a reference to fit the CCA model. We then transformed the remaining cells onto the same canonical correlation space, using this fitted model. The canonical correlation vectors (CCVs) of Xref, and Yref (denoted as Uref, Vref respectively) were computed using UrefSVT ref = XrefYT ref. Subsequently, the CCVs for Xqry and Yqry (Uqry, Vqry respectively) were calculated using Uqry = Xqry ( YT refVref ) =S and Vqry = Yqry ( XT qryUref ) =S. For anchor identification, we concatenated the embeddings from both refer­ ence and query cells. Using the identified anchors, our integration projects the PCs of the query dataset (scm3C-seq), onto the PCs of the reference dataset (snmC-seq), while keeping the PCs of the reference dataset unchanged. This considerably reduced the time and memory consumption for computing the corrected high-dimensional matrix. Our integration approach utilized these an­ chors to project the principal components of the query dataset (scm3C-seq) onto those of the reference dataset (snmC-seq), while preserving the original principal components of the reference dataset. This strategic approach significantly reduced computational demands in terms of both processing time and memory requirements when generating the corrected high-dimensional matrix. The 2-month samples in the snmC-seq dataset originated from the BICCN whole mouse brain dataset, which had previously under­ gone comprehensive annotation. To annotate the remaining cells at the cluster level, we performed two rounds of iterative clustering. This resolution ensured that, for each cluster, >80% of annotated 2-month snmC-seq cells shared the same BICCN subclass label, al­ lowing us to assign that label to all cells in the cluster. Following two rounds of iterative integration, we identified 59 co-clusters during the first iteration and ultimately derived a total of 342 co-clusters after completing the second integration round. For level 1 co-clusters, we applied a straightforward annotation rule: if more than 80% of the 2-month-old mC cells within a co-cluster shared the same sub­ class label, we assigned that label to the entire co-cluster. For co-clusters not meeting this criterion, we based annotations on the cluster information obtained after the second integration round, assigning labels according to the predominant BICCN cell subclass annotation within each cluster. To increase pseudo-bulk coverage for downstream analyses, we merged certain cell subclasses with similar func­ tions and anatomical locations into unified major cell types. Further details of this process are provided in Table S9. Integration of snmC-seq, snm3C-seq, snATAC-seq and 10X Multiome For our integrative analysis, we combined ATAC signals from snATAC-seq and 10X Multiome, and used methylation information from snmC-seq and snm3C-seq. We established snmC-seq data as our reference dataset, with the three other datasets serving as query datasets. PCA on gene-body signals alone was inadequate for capturing the full spectrum of open chromatin heterogeneity present in snATAC-seq data. Previous research42 has shown that Latent Semantic Indexing (LSI) applied to binarized cell-by-5kb bin matrices produces effective embeddings and clustering for snATAC-seq data. Following the previously introduced framework, we imple­ mented this approach using binary sparse cell-by-5kb bin matrices to represent open chromatin regions. We processed snmC-seq data by creating a cell-by-5kb bin matrix and calculating hypomethylation scores using a binomial dis­ tribution model, then binarized the matrix with a 0.9 threshold to produce a sparse binary matrix. Similar processing was applied to snATAC-seq data, where 1 represented at least one read detected in a 5 kb bin in a cell. Dimensionality reduction was performed using LSI with log term frequency and TF-IDF transformation to compute embeddings for both datasets. We then applied CCA with a downsampling framework of 100,000 cells from each dataset as the reference to align the methylation and chromatin acces­ sibility data through anchor identification. Details in ATAC and methylation have been described in our previous work.36 Our code can be found in the "Data and Code Availability" section.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS Age-Differentially DMG analysis To identify Age-Differentially Methylated Genes (DMGs), we employed a reference genome comprising protein-coding and long non- coding RNA genes from the mouse GENCODE vm22 annotation, focusing on gene body regions. We compared methylation patterns across three age groups using single-cell level methylation fractions for both mCH and mCG contexts. For statistical analysis, we applied the Wilcoxon rank-rank test to identify genes with significant methylation changes 2month vs. 18mon, followed by multiple testing corrections using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to adjust p-values. We defined Age-related DMGs as the gene showed significant methylation differences in our Wilcoxon results (FDR < 0.01). The complete analytical pipeline and implementation details are available in our "Data and Code Availability" section.

Age-Differentially DMR analysis Following clustering analysis, we merged individual cell ALLC files into pseudo-bulk ALLC files using the ALLCools.merge-allc com­ mand, grouping cells by cell-type and age. To identify age-related differentially methylated regions (DMRs), we analyzed male and female pseudo-bulk profiles separately within each cell type. We applied a multi-factor model through DMLfit.multiFactor from DSS (version 2.42.0)130 package, with a matrix incorporating cell type, replicate, and age as variables. We then tested specifically for age- related differential methylation sites (DMS) using DMLtest.multiFactor, which implements a beta-binomial regression model opti­ mized for detecting differential methylation signals in methylation datasets. To identify differentially methylated regions (DMRs), we employed the callDMR function and applied rigorous selection criteria, including: (1) p-value < 0.01; (2) A minimum region span of 1 base pair containing at least 1 CpG site; (3) A requirement that at least 50% of CpGs within each candidate region demon­ strate significant differential methylation. Additionally, we merged adjacent DMRs when they were separated by less than 50 base pairs. To identify robust age-associated signals, we further applied two filters: (i) methylation levels had to show a continuous trend with age (keep increasing or decreasing across age groups); and (ii) Δ (max − min) mCG fraction across age groups > 0.20.

To distinguish sex-shared from sex-specific DMRs and avoid misclassifying threshold-dependent signals as sex-specific, we im­ plemented a two-step validation approach. First, we identified DMRs separately in males and females as described above. Second, we merged pseudo-bulk ALLC files from both sexes for each cell type-age combination, normalizing them to ensure equivalent cyto­ sine coverage in both sexes. We then applied the same DMR calling pipeline described above to these merged pseudo-bulk files. DMRs identified in both the sex-separated analyses (i.e., overlapping between males and females) and in the sex-merged analysis were classified as sex-shared, provided that age-related methylation changes occurred in the same direction (both increasing or both decreasing) in males and females. DMRs identified only in the sex-separated analyses were classified as sex-specific. The complete analytical workflow and implementation details are available in the "Data and Code Availability" section. We used whole-genome sequences as the background reference set. For visualization purposes, we applied different filtering criteria to the enrichment results. Genomic Association Tester analysis To assess the association between methylation hotspots and transposable element (TE) subfamilies, we employed the Genomic As­ sociation Tester (GAT) package (v1.3.6), specifically using the gat-run.py function. This statistical framework evaluated whether the identified hotspot regions were significantly enriched for specific TE subfamilies compared to the genomic background. The analysis involved 10,001 permutations, where the algorithm randomly shuffled segments across the whole genome to generate a null distri­ bution of expected overlaps. This permutation-based approach allowed us to determine if the observed overlaps between hotspots and TE subfamily regions occurred at frequencies significantly higher than expected by chance. We established stringent criteria to define significant enrichment: a false discovery rate below 1% (q-value < 0.01) and a fold change (target/background) > 1.1. These thresholds ensured that only robust and biologically meaningful associations between methylation hotspots and TE subfamilies were reported.

Chromatin conformation analysis The chromatin conformation was examined at both individual cell and aggregated levels. For individual cell analysis, we processed data following completion of snm3C-seq mapping and quality filtering procedures outlined in earlier sections. Subsequently, we excluded genomic segments that coincided with the ENCODE blacklist (version 2)123 using ‘‘hicluster filter-contact’’ command. We constructed individual cell chromatin interaction matrices using long-range cis contacts (contact anchor distances > 2,500 bp) and trans contacts at three distinct resolutions: 100-kb for analyzing chromatin compartmentalization; 25-kb for evaluating domain boundary features; and 10-kb for chromatin loop and enhancer analysis. Contact matrix imputation was performed using the scHiCluster118 package (version 1.3.2) using ‘‘hicluster prepare-impute’’ command. This imputation methodology first applied Gaussian convolution (pad = 1) and sub­ sequently employed a random walk with restart algorithm on the convoluted matrix. For 100-kb matrices, imputation was conducted across whole chromosomes, while for 25-kb matrices, imputation was confined to contacts within 10.05 Mb. At 10-kb resolution, impu­ tation was further restricted to contacts within 5.05 Mb. For pseudo-bulk analysis, we merged cells by group (categorized by cell-type, cell-type-age, or cell-type-age-replica). We used the "hicluster merge-cell-raw" command to sum raw matrices or the "hicluster merge- cool" command to calculate the mean of imputed matrices. To maintain analytical rigor in all 3C-related age-differential analyses, we established consistent sampling parameters across groups. We ensured the total number of nuclei between groups is the same and required at least 100 nuclei per group to guarantee sufficient genomic coverage. Contact distance distribution analysis Contact distance distributions for individual cells were generated utilizing the "hicluster contact-distance" command. This created histograms based on the spatial separation between contact anchor pairs. We employed logarithmic binning on a log2 distance scale using 0.125 as the step increment, with the full range extending from 2500 bp to 249 Mb (corresponding to the longest chromosome in the genome). Each histogram bin (denoted as the i-th bin) contained the count of contacts whose distances fell between 2500 × 2(i×0.125) and 2500 × 2((i+1)×0.125). As presented in Figure S10A, we calculated the short-long ratio by dividing the proportion of contacts in bins 51-76 (representing distances from 200kb to 2 Mb) by the proportion found in bins 103-114 (representing dis­ tances from 20 Mb to 50 Mb).

Compartments Analysis For compartment analysis, we employed cell-type-specific pseudo-bulk raw contact matrices for each chromosome at 100 kb res­ olution. We implemented filtering to exclude 100-kb bins exhibiting atypical coverage patterns. Specifically, we defined the coverage of bin i on chromosome c (represented as Rc,i) as the sum of values in the i-th row of the chromosome c contact matrix. We retained only those bins with coverage values falling between the 99th percentile of Rc and a threshold calculated as twice the median of Rc minus the 99th percentile of Rc. Following selection, we normalized the contact matrices by distance and calculated Pearson’s cor­ relation matrices from these normalized datasets.69 These consolidated contact maps served as input for principal component anal­ ysis (PCA) models constructed for individual chromosomes. Domain boundary analysis We identified domains and calculated insulation scores with scHiCluster at 25 kb resolution using ‘‘hicluster domain’’ command. Domain identification for individual cells was performed by applying the TopDom algorithm133 to the imputed matrices at 25 kb res­ olution. For each bin, we calculated insulation scores across each cell-type-age group using pseudo-bulk imputed matrices (derived by averaging across individual cells) and a window size of 10 bins. We defined boundary probability for a specific bin as the fraction of cells within a group where that bin was identified as a domain boundary. To detect differential domain boundaries among the three age groups within each cell type, we constructed a 3×2 contingency table for each 25kb bin, with rows representing the count of cells from each group where the bin was either classified as a boundary or not. Statistical analysis involved calculating the Chi-square statistic and corresponding P value for each bin, with significant differential boundary bins identified using a threshold of FDR < 1 × 10-3. Additionally, we imposed a requirement that the difference between the maximum and minimum boundary proba­ bility is larger than 0.05. In Figures 4D–4F, the CTCF ChIP-seq data from mouse forebrain tissue is obtained from the ENCODE: ENCSR677HXC (https://www.encodeproject.org/experiments/ENCSR677HXC/). Boundary-proximal genes For each age-DB, we applied boundary information generated using the "hicluster domain" command in scHiCluster,118 following the same methodology used for the single-cell files described in the above sections. Briefly, this approach processes pseudobulk-level imputed contact files at 25 kb resolution which are grouped by their cell type and age. For each age-DB, we identified the nearest neighboring boundary in the 2-month-old group. We then intersected genes located within these regions using the GENCODE vM22 GTF file, identifying a set we term "boundary-proximal genes. Detailed code is in the ‘‘Data and Code Availability’’ section. Loop identification and differential loops Chromatin loop detection was performed as previously described.106 Briefly, we identified chromatin loops using the schicluster.­ loop.call_loop function for each cell-type-age or cell-type-region-age group. Chromatin loop identification was conducted within the 50 kb to 5 Mb range. The process involved transforming imputed chromosome matrices (Q) through log-transformation and Z-score normalization at each diagonal (yielding E), followed by subtraction of a local background between ≥30 kb and ≤50 kb (pro­ ducing T). Statistical significance was established using t-statistics to quantify how significantly E and T deviated from zero across cells. Differential loops were identified in two comparisons: between all age groups within the same cell type, and between age groups within cell-type-region categories. To compare loop interaction strength between different cell groups, we performed ANOVA to compute the statistical difference for each loop identified in at least one age group. Age-differential loops were defined as the top 15% of loops based on ANOVA values. Details can be found in the ‘‘Data and Code Availability’’ section. Activity by Contact analysis We employed the Activity-by-Contact (ABC) model134 to identify putative enhancers and their target genes in each group. Briefly, the ABC model integrates pseudo-bulk raw contact frequencies from 3C data with enhancer activity measurements to predict enhancer- gene pairs. We identified candidate cis-regulatory elements (cCREs) using two methods: (1) ATAC-seq peaks from the correspond­ ing cell-type-age group, and (2) cell-type-specific hypomethylated regions (hypo-DMRs; mCG fraction < 0.7) from the corresponding cell type.36 Enhancer activity was quantified using RPKM values for ATAC-seq data and (1 - mCG fraction) for methylation data. Pre­ dictions with an ABC score ≥0.02 were classified as positive interactions and used for downstream analysis. Detailed code can be found in the ‘‘Data and Code Availability’’ section.

MERFISH and age-DEG analysis To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with the MERFISH dataset, we grouped cells by their coronal slices and compared across age groups within each cell type. The identification of DEGs involved the following steps. We filtered cells with less than 10 genes expressed and genes with less than 5 cells expressed. For each sample, the data were normalized to total counts using scan­ py.normalize_total() function and log-scaled using scanpy.pp.log1p(). We performed two types of statistical tests. First, we per­ formed the Kruskal-Wallis test, a non-parametric alternative to ANOVA, to identify genes with significant expression differences across three different groups simultaneously. We also compared DEGs pairwise among three age groups, where we conducted pair­ wise comparisons using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (implemented in the diff_ranksum function) for all possible sample group com­ binations. For both methods, the p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method, controlling for type I errors while maintaining statistical power. Here we introduce how we generated the input dataset, with detailed code in the ‘‘Data and Code Availability’’ section. Our model integrates three epigenetic modalities across seven features (X) to predict age-related gene expression changes (Y). The input data generation process is described below: X: For methylation modality, three different methylation features were extracted: 1) Genebody DMRs: For each gene, we collected all age-DMRs located within the gene body ± 2kb. Input features included DMR mCG methylation levels at 2mo, 9mo, and 18mo time­ points, gene length, distance to the transcription start site (TSS), DMR length, and additional relevant parameters. 2) Gene body mCG fraction: We calculated the average mCG fraction across the gene body ± 2kb at 2 month, 9 month, and 18 month timepoints. Input features included these methylation levels, gene length, and p-values from age-related differentially methylated gene (DMG) anal­ ysis. 3) Gene body mCH fraction: Identical parameters as mCG fraction analysis but using non-CpG (mCH) methylation levels instead. EAT model We developed a transformer-based model, EpiAgingTransformer (EAT). The model starts with seven transformer modules, followed by a self-attention aggregator and a multiple-layer perceptron (MLP) classifier. We used the sequence dataset (7 epigenetic feature matrices, where in each matrix the rows represent different genomic loci and the columns are the features associated with each loci) to train the EAT model.
